Part 3 — Rings and Fields

A ring is where two compositions negotiate with each other. Addition behaves like an Abelian group; multiplication behaves like a monoid; distributivity is the treaty that lets the two operations share structure.

1. Distributivity as area

In a group there was only one operation. In a ring there are two: + and ×. The point is not merely that both exist; the point is that multiplication distributes over addition.

The rectangle below is the tactile model. One side has length a; the other side is split into b+c. Moving the split never changes the equality a(b+c)=ab+ac; it only changes how the total area decomposes.

DefinitionA ring is a set R with addition and multiplication such that R is an Abelian group under addition, a monoid under multiplication, and multiplication distributes over addition on both sides.
Where the picture liesThe area model is exact for ordinary nonnegative real lengths. For an abstract ring it is a faithful memory aid for the law, not a literal geometry of arbitrary elements.

2. The clock returns as a ring

Part 1 introduced ℤ/mℤ as a wrap-around world. Now the same clock carries both addition and multiplication. Addition is always group-like, but multiplication can fail dramatically: nonzero elements may multiply to zero.

That failure is the first ring-specific obstruction. A nonzero element that kills another nonzero element is a zero-divisor. An element with a multiplicative inverse is a unit. When m is prime, every nonzero residue becomes green: ℤ/pℤ is a field.

TheoremIn ℤ/mℤ, a residue [a] has a multiplicative inverse exactly when a is coprime to m. Consequently ℤ/mℤ is a field exactly when m is prime.
Where the picture liesThe clock and the table are exact for the displayed finite ring. The red/green coloring is not metaphorical: it is computed by gcd(a,m).

3. Ideals as absorbing sets

A subgroup of the additive group of a ring is not yet enough to form a quotient ring. The subset must also be stable under multiplication by arbitrary ring elements. That is the absorption condition.

On the integer line, the principal ideal (a)=aℤ is the set of all multiples of a. Choose an element inside it, multiply it by any integer r, and it remains inside. This is the ring-theoretic analogue of a normal subgroup: exactly the kind of subset whose cosets can inherit structure.

DefinitionAn ideal I⊂R is an additive subgroup such that x∈I implies rx∈I and xr∈I for every r∈R. The quotient R/I inherits a ring structure.
Where the picture liesFor ideals (a) in ℤ, the multiples picture is exact. The red subset is a contrast object, not an ideal. Quotient wrapping to ℤ/aℤ is exact for ℤ/(a).

4. Prime vs maximal, via the quotient

Ideals become easier to classify by looking at what their quotients remember. A prime ideal is one whose quotient has no zero-divisors. A maximal ideal is one whose quotient is a field. Thus “prime” and “maximal” are properties of the collapsed world.

Inside ℤ/12ℤ, choose an ideal generated by a divisor d of 12. The quotient behaves like ℤ/dℤ. When that quotient is a field, the ideal is maximal; when it is merely an integral domain, the ideal is prime. In these finite commutative examples, those lights turn on together.

TheoremFor a commutative ring R and ideal I: I is prime iff R/I is an integral domain. I is maximal iff R/I is a field. Maximal implies prime.
Where the picture liesThis figure is exact for the ideals of ℤ/12ℤ generated by divisors of 12. It does not assert that all prime ideals are maximal in arbitrary rings.

5. Polynomials as coefficient vectors

A polynomial is not just a function; in algebra it is a formal finite list of coefficients. Addition adds lists. Multiplication convolves lists. Over a field F, degrees behave well: nonzero leading coefficients cannot multiply to zero.

TheoremIf R is entire, then R[X] is entire and deg(fg)=deg(f)+deg(g) for nonzero polynomials f,g. In particular, F[X] is an integral domain for every field F.
Where the picture liesThe coefficient-vector model is exact for formal polynomials of the displayed degree bound. The canvas truncates input degrees to keep the multiplication visible.

6. The Euclidean algorithm, animated — twice

The same algorithm lives in two different rings. In ℤ, divide integers and keep remainders. In F[X], divide polynomials and keep lower-degree remainders. The common structure is Euclidean: every division step makes a norm smaller.

TheoremEvery Euclidean ring is a PID, every PID is a UFD, and therefore every Euclidean ring is a UFD. For any field F, F[X] is Euclidean using degree.
Where the picture liesThe displayed computations are exact. The side-by-side rhythm is the metaphor: the same proof schema applies because both rings admit division with smaller remainder.

7. The UFD ⊃ PID ⊃ Euclidean nesting

The algebraic properties form a hierarchy. Euclidean domains have an algorithm. Principal ideal domains have every ideal generated by one element. Unique factorization domains have irreducible atoms with unique decompositions. Each implication goes outward.

TheoremEuclidean domain ⇒ PID ⇒ UFD. In a PID, irreducible elements are prime; in a UFD, complete factorizations into irreducibles are unique up to associates and ordering.
Where the picture liesThe containments are exact. The named rings are illustrative landmarks; the nested ovals do not encode all integral domains or all possible refinements.

8. A periodic table per field

In F[X], irreducible polynomials are the atoms. Change the coefficient field and the periodic table changes. Over ℂ, every atom is linear. Over ℝ, atoms are linear or quadratic. Over finite fields and ℚ, the table becomes rich.

TheoremFor a field F, F[X] is a UFD. The irreducible polynomials are therefore the multiplicative atoms of polynomial factorization over F.
Where the picture liesThe tiles are representative, not exhaustive. The statements “ℂ has only linear irreducibles” and “ℝ has only linear and irreducible quadratic irreducibles” are exact; the finite-field and ℚ panels show finite samples.
