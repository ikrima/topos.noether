Rings and Fields

A group has one operation. But the structures that model arithmetic — the integers, the rationals, the real and complex numbers — each carry two: addition and multiplication, bound together by distributivity. A ring is exactly this: a group under addition, a monoid under multiplication, and a law connecting them. The moment we add the second operation, the theory deepens. Ideals appear, quotients multiply, and a new kind of atom — the irreducible polynomial — takes the stage.

This is Part 3 of the series companion to Paulin's Introduction to Abstract Algebra, covering Chapter 4. Every recurring thread returns: the clock from Part 1 becomes a ring with two operation tables; the cosets of Part 2 become ideal cosets and quotient rings; the atoms of prime factorisation become irreducible polynomials. By the end, we will have the tools — UFDs, PIDs, and the Euclidean algorithm — needed to attack the field extensions of Part 4.

1. The ring axioms

A ring is a set $R$ equipped with two operations, $+$ and $\cdot$, such that $(R, +)$ is an Abelian group, $(R, \cdot)$ is a monoid (associative with identity), and the two are linked by distributivity. The identity for $+$ is written $0$; the identity for $\cdot$ is written $1$.

Definition — Ring

A ring is a triple $(R, +, \cdot)$ where:

Additive group: $(R, +)$ is an Abelian group (associative, commutative, identity $0$, inverses $-a$).
Multiplicative monoid: $(R, \cdot)$ is a monoid (associative, identity $1$).
Distributivity: $a \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c$ and $(a + b) \cdot c = a \cdot c + b \cdot c$.

If $\cdot$ is commutative ($ab = ba$), $R$ is a commutative ring. We require $1 \neq 0$ (excluding the trivial ring) unless stated otherwise.

You already know the canonical examples. $(\mathbb{Z}, +, \times)$ is a commutative ring. So is $(\mathbb{Q}, +, \times)$, $(\mathbb{R}, +, \times)$, and $(\mathbb{C}, +, \times)$. The integers modulo $m$, $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$, form a commutative ring — the clock from Part 1, now recognised as carrying both operations. Matrix rings $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ are non-commutative rings. Polynomial rings $R[X]$ — formal expressions $\sum a_i X^i$ with coefficients in $R$ — are commutative when $R$ is. The last is our workhorse for Part 4, so we develop it carefully below.

The two operations are not symmetric: addition is a group (every element has an inverse), but multiplication is only a monoid (inverses need not exist). The elements that do have multiplicative inverses are the units; the elements that produce zero when multiplied by a nonzero partner are the zero-divisors. A ring with no zero-divisors is an integral domain, and it is the closest a ring can come to behaving like $\mathbb{Z}$.

Notice that…

The interaction law — distributivity — is what makes a ring a ring. Without it, $+$ and $\cdot$ would be two independent structures sharing a set. Distributivity is the single axiom that ties them together, and it is the source of essentially every deep theorem in the subject: the binomial theorem, the Euclidean algorithm, unique factorisation. The area picture is exact for non-negative real $a, b, c$: distributivity is literally a statement about areas, and the rectangle partition captures it without metaphor. What the picture does not capture is the general case — negative numbers, matrices, polynomials, or elements of $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ — where "area" has no geometric meaning but the algebraic law still holds. The figure is an intuition pump for the axiom, not a proof of it in full generality.

2. The clock returns as a ring

The clock from Part 1 — $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ — was our first quotient structure, and we used it to show that $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field exactly when $p$ is prime. Now we revisit it with both operations on equal footing. The clock is a ring: addition is the wrap-around from Part 1, multiplication is repeated stepping, and the two interact via distributivity. The distinction between units and zero-divisors — invisible when we only had addition — becomes the central structural feature.

Figure 2 — The clock returns as a ring. The dial (left) colours each residue green if it is a unit (coprime to $m$) or red if it is a zero-divisor. The two operation tables (right) show $+$ and $\times$ side by side, with the same colour coding in their headers. The integral-domain status light turns green only when $m$ is prime — no zero-divisors, every nonzero element a unit, hence a field.

Definition — Integral Domain, Unit, Zero-divisor

Let $R$ be a commutative ring.

Zero-divisor: a nonzero $a \in R$ such that $ab = 0$ for some nonzero $b \in R$.
Unit: $a \in R$ with a multiplicative inverse — $ab = 1$ for some $b$. The units form a group $R^\times$.
Integral domain: a nonzero commutative ring with no zero-divisors (if $ab = 0$ then $a = 0$ or $b = 0$).

Notice that…

Slide $m$ to $6$. The red residues are $\{2, 3, 4\}$ — each shares a factor with $6$, and each is a zero-divisor ($2 \cdot 3 = 0$, $3 \cdot 2 = 0$, $4 \cdot 3 = 0$). The green residues are $\{1, 5\}$ — coprime to $6$, hence units. Now slide to $7$: every nonzero residue turns green. There are no zero-divisors, because $7$ is prime and $\gcd(a, 7) = 1$ for all $1 \leq a \leq 6$. The ring $\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain — and, since it is finite, a field. This is the same theorem from Part 1, now seen through the lens of the two-operation structure.

Theorem — Finite Integral Domain ⇒ Field

Every finite integral domain is a field. For if $a \neq 0$ and $R$ is finite, the map $x \mapsto ax$ is injective (since $ax = ay \Rightarrow a(x-y) = 0 \Rightarrow x = y$ by the domain property), hence surjective, so $1$ is in the image and $a$ has an inverse. In particular, $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field $\Leftrightarrow$ $p$ is prime (Part 1's theorem, restated).

3. Ideals: the absorbing sets

The clock $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ is a quotient: we divided $\mathbb{Z}$ by the multiples of $m$. The multiples of $m$ are not just a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ — they are an ideal, a subgroup that also absorbs multiplication by anything in the ring. This absorption property is what makes the quotient a ring, not just a group. Ideals are to rings what normal subgroups are to groups: the things you can divide by.

Definition — Ideal

A subset $I \subseteq R$ is an ideal if:

Additive subgroup: $(I, +)$ is a subgroup of $(R, +)$ (closed under $+$ and $-$, contains $0$).
Absorption: for all $r \in R$ and $a \in I$, both $ra \in I$ and $ar \in I$.

The ideal generated by $a \in R$ is $(a) = \{ra : r \in R\}$ — all multiples of $a$. An ideal of this form is principal.

In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ mode, the ideal $(a + bi)$ is a square lattice rotated and scaled by $|a + bi|$. Switch to $(3, 0)$ — you get a square grid with spacing $3$. Switch to $(1+i)$ — the grid is rotated $45°$ and scaled by $\sqrt{2}$. Every ideal in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is principal (it is a PID), so every ideal is a rotated lattice. The absorption property is visible: no matter how far out you go, multiplying a lattice point by any Gaussian integer lands you on another lattice point. The lattice is closed under external multiplication — that is what "ideal" means.

Where the picture lies

The lattice picture of an ideal in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is exact — ideals in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ are genuinely 2D lattices, and absorption is a literal geometric closure property. What the figure does not generalise is to arbitrary rings: in a ring like $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, ideals are not geometric lattices but algebraic sets of polynomials. The figure is exact for $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (both PIDs with a Euclidean structure); it is suggestive, not literal, for general rings. The integer-line view of $(a) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ is also exact — the evenly-spaced dots are literally the multiples of $a$.

The quotient $R/I$ is formed exactly as in Part 2: cosets of $I$ in $R$ (under addition), with $(a + I)(b + I) = ab + I$. Absorption is what makes this well-defined: if $a' = a + i_1$ and $b' = b + i_2$ (same cosets), then $a'b' = ab + ai_2 + i_1 b + i_1 i_2$, and each of the last three terms lies in $I$ by absorption — so $a'b' + I = ab + I$. The quotient is a ring, and the clock $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ is the quotient of $\mathbb{Z}$ by the ideal $(m)$.

4. Prime and maximal ideals

Two special kinds of ideal control the structure of the quotient. An ideal $I$ is prime if $ab \in I$ implies $a \in I$ or $b \in I$ — the quotient $R/I$ has no zero-divisors, hence is an integral domain. An ideal $I$ is maximal if it is not contained in any larger proper ideal — the quotient $R/I$ has no proper ideals, hence (if $R/I$ is commutative) is a field. Every maximal ideal is prime; the converse fails. The figure below lets you explore both.

Definition — Prime and Maximal Ideals

Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $I \subsetneq R$ a proper ideal.

Prime: $ab \in I \Rightarrow a \in I$ or $b \in I$. Equivalently, $R/I$ is an integral domain.
Maximal: if $I \subseteq J \subseteq R$ for an ideal $J$, then $J = I$ or $J = R$. Equivalently, $R/I$ is a field.

Every maximal ideal is prime. The converse holds in PIDs (next section) but not in general.

In $\mathbb{Z}/12$, the ideals are $(0), (2), (3), (4), (6), (1) = \mathbb{Z}/12$. Pick $I = (3)$: the quotient has $4$ elements $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$, and $2 \cdot 2 = 1$ (mod $3$ in the quotient) — every nonzero element has an inverse, so the quotient is a field and $I$ is maximal. Now pick $I = (4)$: the quotient has $4$ elements, but $2 \cdot 2 = 0$ — a zero-divisor! So $I$ is not prime (and not maximal). The chain $(0) \subset (6) \subset (3) \subset (1)$ shows containment: maximal ideals are the ones with nothing above them but the whole ring.

5. Polynomial rings

Given a ring $R$, the polynomial ring $R[X]$ consists of formal sums $\sum_{i=0}^n a_i X^i$ with $a_i \in R$. Addition is coefficient-wise; multiplication is the convolution — the coefficient of $X^k$ in $fg$ is $\sum_{i+j=k} f_i g_j$. The degree $\deg(f)$ is the highest index with a nonzero coefficient (and $\deg(0) = -\infty$ by convention). A key property: if $R$ is an integral domain, then $\deg(fg) = \deg(f) + \deg(g)$, so $R[X]$ is also an integral domain.

Notice that…

The convolution is a "sliding dot product": to get the $k$-th coefficient of $fg$, reverse $f$, slide it along $g$, and sum the products of overlapping coefficients. This is exactly how integer multiplication works digit-by-digit (with carries suppressed). The degree-additivity $\deg(fg) = \deg(f) + \deg(g)$ is visible: the highest nonzero term of $fg$ comes from the highest term of $f$ times the highest term of $g$, and since $\mathbb{F}_p$ has no zero-divisors, their product is nonzero. Over a ring with zero-divisors, the leading coefficients could cancel and the degree could drop — which is why "integral domain" matters.

Two structural facts about $R[X]$ will be load-bearing in Part 4. First, if $R$ is a field $F$, then $F[X]$ is a Euclidean domain (Section 6) — it has a division algorithm — and hence a PID and a UFD. This makes $F[X]$ behave like $\mathbb{Z}$, and the analogy is exact and powerful. Second, the quotient $F[X]/(p)$, for an irreducible polynomial $p$, is a field extension of $F$ — this is how we will construct $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ and the splitting fields of Part 4. The irreducibles of $F[X]$ are the "atoms," and classifying them is the periodic-table problem of Section 8.

6. The Euclidean algorithm

A ring $R$ is a Euclidean domain if it admits a "size" function $\delta : R \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{N}$ such that for any $a, b \neq 0$, we can write $a = bq + r$ with $r = 0$ or $\delta(r) < \delta(b)$. The two examples that matter are $\mathbb{Z}$ (with $\delta = |n|$) and $F[X]$ (with $\delta = \deg$). Both admit the Euclidean algorithm for GCD, and both are therefore PIDs and UFDs. The visual claim of the figure below is that $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[X]$ are the same kind of ring — the algorithm is identical in rhythm, only the "size" differs.

Theorem — Euclidean ⇒ PID ⇒ UFD

The containments are strict: every Euclidean domain is a PID, every PID is a UFD, and the converses fail. In a Euclidean domain, the Euclidean algorithm computes $\gcd$ in finitely many steps (the size strictly decreases). In a PID, every ideal is principal, and irreducibles are prime — which gives unique factorisation. $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[X]$ are Euclidean; $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is Euclidean (with norm $N(a+bi) = a^2+b^2$); $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not even a UFD.

Notice that…

On the left, the rectangle-tiling shows $\gcd(48, 18)$: $48 = 18 \cdot 2 + 12$, then $18 = 12 \cdot 1 + 6$, then $12 = 6 \cdot 2 + 0$. The last nonzero remainder is $6$, which is the side of the largest square tiling the rectangle. On the right, polynomial division over $\mathbb{F}_3$ follows the identical rhythm: divide leading terms, multiply back, subtract, repeat. The degree strictly decreases at each step — the exact analogue of the integer size decreasing. The two algorithms are literally the same algorithm in two different Euclidean domains.

7. UFD, PID, and Euclidean nesting

The three classes of "nice" commutative rings nest: Euclidean $\subset$ PID $\subset$ UFD. Each inclusion is strict, and the boundary cases are where the theory gets interesting. The figure below drops a token into each region and names a ring that lives exactly there, with a failure case that prevents it from sinking deeper.

Figure 7 — The UFD $\supset$ PID $\supset$ Euclidean nesting. Three concentric regions: the innermost (green) is Euclidean domains; the middle (amber) is PIDs that are not Euclidean; the outermost (blue) is UFDs that are not PIDs. Each region hosts a representative ring and a failure case showing why it cannot sink deeper. ℤ[√−5] sits outside the UFD region entirely (red) — here irreducible does not imply prime, and unique factorisation fails.

Definition — UFD, PID, Euclidean

UFD (Unique Factorisation Domain): every nonzero non-unit factors into irreducibles, uniquely up to order and units. Here "irreducible = prime."

PID (Principal Ideal Domain): an integral domain where every ideal is principal.

Euclidean domain: admits a Euclidean size function $\delta$ enabling division with remainder.

Euclidean $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD. All three are integral domains. In a PID (hence Euclidean), irreducible $\Leftrightarrow$ prime; in a UFD this holds but is part of the definition.

Where the picture lies

The containment nesting is exact — the inclusions Euclidean $\subset$ PID $\subset$ UFD are theorems, and each is strict. What the figure does not capture is the size of each region: most UFDs are not PIDs, and most PIDs are not Euclidean, but the picture draws them as similar-sized rings for legibility. The token rings ($\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, $F[X]$, $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$) are illustrative examples, not a classification. The $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ failure ($6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$, two distinct factorisations into irreducibles) is the classical witness that "irreducible $\neq$ prime" outside UFDs.

The distinction between "irreducible" and "prime" is the technical heart of this section. In any integral domain, prime $\Rightarrow$ irreducible (easy). The converse holds in UFDs (hard — it is essentially what unique factorisation says), but fails in general. In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, the element $2$ is irreducible (cannot be factored further), but it is not prime: $2 \mid 6 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$, but $2$ divides neither factor. This is the obstruction that prevents $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ from being a UFD, and it is why we insist on UFDs when we want unique factorisation. The Euclidean algorithm, when it exists, bypasses all of this by giving a constructive GCD — which is why $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[X]$ are so well-behaved.

Two further tools for proving irreducibility, both essential in Part 4, deserve mention. Gauss's Lemma states that a primitive polynomial (one whose coefficients have gcd $1$) over a UFD $R$ is irreducible over $R$ iff it is irreducible over the field of fractions $\mathrm{Frac}(R)$ — this lets us transfer irreducibility questions from $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ to $\mathbb{Q}[X]$. Eisenstein's criterion gives a sufficient condition: if $p$ is prime and $p \mid a_i$ for $i < n$ but $p^2 \nmid a_0$ and $p \nmid a_n$, then $\sum a_i X^i$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. Eisenstein produces irreducibles of every degree over $\mathbb{Q}$ — the seed of the "bewildering complexity" you will see in Figure 8.

8. A periodic table per field

The irreducibles of $F[X]$ are the atoms of polynomial arithmetic. Over a field $F$, every polynomial factors uniquely into irreducibles (since $F[X]$ is a UFD), and the shape of the irreducible set depends dramatically on $F$. Notice that…

Over $\mathbb{C}$, the table is trivial: every irreducible is linear, because every non-constant polynomial has a root (the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra). Over $\mathbb{R}$, irreducibles are linear or quadratic (with negative discriminant) — a consequence of $\mathbb{C}$ being a degree-$2$ algebraic extension. Over $\mathbb{F}_p$, the count of irreducibles of degree $d$ is $\frac{1}{d}\sum_{e \mid d} \mu(e) p^{d/e}$ (a formula from Möbius inversion) — finite and computable. Over $\mathbb{Q}$, the count in each degree is infinite: Eisenstein's criterion, applied to $X^n - p$ for any prime $p$, gives an irreducible of degree $n$ for every $n$. The wildness over $\mathbb{Q}$ is exactly why the quintic is interesting — there are too many atoms to classify by hand.

Theorem — Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Every non-constant polynomial with complex coefficients has a root in $\mathbb{C}$. Equivalently, $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed: the only irreducible polynomials in $\mathbb{C}[X]$ are the linear ones. This is why the $\mathbb{C}$ row of the periodic table is trivial, and it is the reason every polynomial splits completely over $\mathbb{C}$ — the splitting field of Part 4 always lives inside $\mathbb{C}$ (for polynomials over $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{C}$).

Where the picture lies

The periodic table is exact as a census — the counts of irreducibles per degree over $\mathbb{F}_p$ are given by a precise formula, and the qualitative claims over $\mathbb{C}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{Q}$ are theorems. What the figure does not show is the actual irreducibles (there are too many, especially over $\mathbb{Q}$); it shows a few representatives per degree. The ordering within a row is arbitrary — there is no canonical "periodic" ordering of irreducibles, unlike Mendeleev's table. The "atom" metaphor is exact: irreducibles are the multiplicative atoms of $F[X]$ by unique factorisation, just as primes are the atoms of $\mathbb{Z}$.

The recurring threads are now fully interwoven with the two-operation structure. The clock from Part 1 returned as a ring with two operation tables; its zero-divisors and units are now first-class objects. The Cayley table from Part 2 became the multiplication table of a quotient ring $R/I$. The coloring by partition became ideal-coset coloring, and the quotient construction is identical to Part 2's group quotient — ideals are the ring-theoretic analogue of normal subgroups. The atoms from prime factorisation became irreducible polynomials, classified per field in the periodic table. And the structure-preserving maps — functions and bijections in Part 1, homomorphisms in Part 2 — are now ring homomorphisms, with kernels that are ideals and images that are subrings. The First Isomorphism Theorem for rings ($R/\ker\varphi \cong \mathrm{im}\varphi$) is the same theorem as Part 2's, restated for the two-operation setting.
