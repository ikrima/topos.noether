Field Extensions and Galois Theory

A companion to Alexander Paulin's Introduction to Abstract Algebra, Chapter 5. The finale.

Three parts of scaffolding have brought us here. We know what a group is, what a ring is, what a field is. We know what atoms look like: primes in $\mathbb{Z}$, simple groups in group theory, irreducibles in $F[X]$. Now the four are going to talk to each other. A single irreducible polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ will build a field. That field will carry a group of symmetries. And that group's atoms will decide whether the polynomial has a formula in radicals — for degree 4, yes; for degree 5, in general, no. The reason is $A_5$, planted at the end of Part 2, waiting.

§1 A field extension is a new dimension

Fix a field $F$. A field extension $E/F$ is a field $E$ that contains $F$ as a subfield. Everything in $E$ can be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided; and the operations on elements of $F$ are the ones you already knew. The key insight, which lets us actually compute, is that $E$ can be viewed as a vector space over $F$ — because $F$-scalars act on $E$-elements exactly the way scalars act on vectors.

Definition · degree of an extension
For a field extension $E/F$, the degree $[E:F]$ is the dimension of $E$ as an $F$-vector space. If $[E:F]$ is finite, we call $E/F$ a finite extension.

The smallest genuinely new example: adjoin $\sqrt{2}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$. The resulting field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ has the elements $a + b\sqrt{2}$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$. That is a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space of dimension $2$, with basis $\{1, \sqrt{2}\}$. Draw it as a plane. Then multiplying by a fixed element becomes an ordinary $2 \times 2$ matrix acting on the plane — a linear map, using the linear-algebra reflex you have been cultivating for years.

Concretely for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$: multiplication by $\alpha = a + b\sqrt{2}$ sends $1 \mapsto a + b\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{2} \mapsto 2b + a\sqrt{2}$. As a matrix on the basis $\{1, \sqrt{2}\}$, $$ M_\alpha = \begin{pmatrix} a & 2b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}. $$ The determinant, $a^2 - 2b^2$, is the norm $N_{E/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)$ — and it is nonzero precisely when $\alpha \neq 0$, which is why inverses exist in $E$: $M_\alpha$ is invertible.

Theorem · tower law (exact)
If $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$ is a tower of finite extensions, then $[E:F] = [E:K] \cdot [K:F]$.

The tower law is Part 2's Lagrange in disguise: a vector-space-dimension identity that will let us count subfields the way we once counted cosets.

§2 Algebraic elements and the minimal polynomial

Pick a specific element $\alpha \in E$. Look at its powers: $1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \alpha^3, \ldots$ Two things can happen. Either these are all $F$-linearly independent (there is no polynomial with coefficients in $F$ vanishing at $\alpha$) — we call $\alpha$ transcendental. Or eventually one of the powers is a linear combination of the earlier ones — the powers collapse, and we call $\alpha$ algebraic.

Definition · minimal polynomial
For $\alpha \in E$ algebraic over $F$, the minimal polynomial $m_\alpha(X) \in F[X]$ is the unique monic polynomial of smallest degree with $m_\alpha(\alpha) = 0$. It is irreducible in $F[X]$, and every $g(X) \in F[X]$ vanishing at $\alpha$ is divisible by $m_\alpha$.
Theorem · what adjoining an element looks like
If $\alpha$ is algebraic over $F$ with minimal polynomial of degree $n$, then
  • $F(\alpha) = F[\alpha]$ (adjoining $\alpha$ closes under $\times$ and division automatically),
  • $\{1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \ldots, \alpha^{n-1}\}$ is an $F$-basis of $F(\alpha)$,
  • and hence $[F(\alpha):F] = \deg m_\alpha$.

Two connections back. First: the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ is an irreducible element of $F[X]$ (Part 3's atom). Adjoining $\alpha$ to $F$ is the same as forming the quotient ring $F[X] / (m_\alpha)$ — which is a field precisely because $(m_\alpha)$ is a maximal ideal, and $(m_\alpha)$ is maximal precisely because $m_\alpha$ is irreducible. That is the entire content of Part 3, doing exactly one job.

§3 Splitting fields and roots

Adjoining one root of a polynomial gives us a field, but not necessarily a field containing all of the polynomial's roots. For that we adjoin them all. Watch what happens in $\mathbb{C}$ as we drag polynomial coefficients: the roots dance in the plane. The splitting field is the smallest field over $F$ that contains every dancer.

Definition · splitting field
A splitting field for $f \in F[X]$ is a field extension $E/F$ such that
  • $f$ factors into linear factors in $E[X]$, and
  • $E$ is generated over $F$ by the roots of $f$.
Splitting fields exist and are unique up to isomorphism over $F$.

Two examples worth noticing. The splitting field of $x^2 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$: adjoining one root gives both, since the other is $-\sqrt{2}$. The splitting field of $x^3 - 2$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ where $\omega = e^{2\pi i /3}$: adjoining the real cube root is not enough because the two complex cube roots require the primitive third root of unity. The degrees are $2$ and $6$ respectively — count them from the picture: $2 = 2$ conjugate roots, $6 = 3\cdot 2$ (three cube roots, joined by rotation through $\omega$).

§4 The Galois group: symmetries of the roots

Here is the central idea of the whole subject. Suppose $E$ is the splitting field of some polynomial $f \in F[X]$. Consider all field automorphisms of $E$ that fix $F$ pointwise — every element of $F$ is left alone; only elements of $E \setminus F$ can be moved. These automorphisms form a group under composition.

Definition · Galois group
For a field extension $E/F$, the Galois group is $$ \mathrm{Gal}(E/F) = \{ \sigma : E \to E \mid \sigma \text{ is a field automorphism and } \sigma(x) = x \text{ for all } x \in F \}. $$

Now watch. If $\sigma \in \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ and $\alpha$ is a root of $f \in F[X]$, then $\sigma(\alpha)$ is also a root of $f$ — because $$ 0 = \sigma(0) = \sigma(f(\alpha)) = f(\sigma(\alpha)) $$ using only that $\sigma$ is a field homomorphism and that $f$ has coefficients in $F$ (which $\sigma$ fixes). So every element of the Galois group permutes the roots of every $F$-polynomial. Because $E$ is generated over $F$ by the roots, this permutation determines $\sigma$ completely.

This is where all the algebra we've built becomes visual. The roots sit in the complex plane. Between them run relations — algebraic equations they satisfy that are inherited from $F$. Some relations are automatic (like being conjugate); others come from the specific polynomial (like being a root of $f$); others express particular products or sums of roots equaling elements of $F$. Every relation must be respected. Any permutation of the roots that preserves all the relations is in the Galois group. Any permutation that breaks one is not.

Notice how few permutations survive. For $x^3 - 2$, there are $3! = 6$ permutations of the three roots, and — remarkably — all six preserve the relations, giving $\mathrm{Gal} \cong \mathrm{Sym}_3$. For $x^4 - 1$, there are $4! = 24$ permutations of the roots $\{1, i, -1, -i\}$, but only two preserve the relations $1 \cdot 1 = 1$, $i^2 = -1$, and so on, giving $\mathrm{Gal} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ (identity and complex conjugation). The Galois group is a subgroup of $\mathrm{Sym}_n$ — and typically a small one.

Theorem · size of the Galois group
If $E/F$ is a finite Galois extension (that is, the splitting field of a separable polynomial), then $$ |\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)| = [E:F]. $$ Moreover, $\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ acts faithfully on the roots of $f$, and if $f$ is irreducible of degree $n$, then $\mathrm{Gal}(E/F) \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Sym}_n$.
Where the picture lies
We are drawing only the roots and a few "structural" relations (conjugate pairs, product/sum invariants). The full set of $F$-relations among the roots is generally infinite (any polynomial identity holds), and computing $\mathrm{Gal}$ from scratch is nontrivial. The figure faithfully depicts the permutation action; determining which permutations qualify is precisely the labor of Galois theory. Also: we are silently assuming characteristic 0 (i.e. $\mathrm{char}\,F = 0$), where every irreducible polynomial is automatically separable. In characteristic $p$ there is a subtlety Paulin discusses.

§5 The Galois correspondence

The astonishing fact — the fundamental theorem of the whole subject — is that the algebraic anatomy of a Galois extension $E/F$ is encoded, faithfully and reversibly, in its Galois group.

Theorem · Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory
Let $E/F$ be a finite Galois extension with Galois group $G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$. Then there is a bijection $$ \{\text{intermediate fields } F \subseteq K \subseteq E\} \;\;\longleftrightarrow\;\; \{\text{subgroups } H \leq G\} $$ given by $$ K \;\longmapsto\; \mathrm{Gal}(E/K), \qquad H \;\longmapsto\; E^H := \{x \in E : \sigma(x) = x \text{ for all } \sigma \in H\}. $$ This bijection is
  • inclusion-reversing: bigger field ⇔ smaller subgroup;
  • degree-preserving: $[E:K] = |\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)|$ and $[K:F] = [G : \mathrm{Gal}(E/K)]$;
  • normality-preserving: $K/F$ is a normal extension iff $\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, in which case $\mathrm{Gal}(K/F) \cong G / \mathrm{Gal}(E/K)$.

This deserves to be seen as two mirror lattices — one of subfields, one of subgroups, drawn side by side. Below, the worked example is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)/\mathbb{Q}$, whose Galois group is $\mathrm{Sym}_3$ (an old friend from Part 2). The correspondence is a machine: an intractable question about subfields (which are there? how do they nest? is $K/F$ Galois?) is turned into a question about subgroups of a finite group (which we can answer with the tools of Part 2). Every classical impossibility proof — trisecting the angle, doubling the cube, squaring the circle, and the one we are about to reach — reduces to arithmetic in a specific finite group.

§6 Solvability by radicals

A polynomial equation is solvable by radicals if its roots can be written using $+, -, \times, \div$ and $n$th roots ($\sqrt[n]{\cdot}$) starting from the coefficients. For degree $2$ (the quadratic formula), $3$ (Cardano), and $4$ (Ferrari), such formulas were known by the mid-16th century. For degree $5$, three centuries of searching produced nothing — until Ruffini and Abel proved no such formula exists, and Galois told us why.

The mechanism is a translation. Extracting an $n$th root corresponds, on the field side, to a very special kind of extension: adjoining a root of $x^n - c$, which (once we throw in a primitive $n$th root of unity) is a Galois extension with cyclic Galois group $\mathbb{Z}/n$. Building the root formula step by step therefore corresponds to a tower of cyclic Galois extensions. The Galois group of the whole thing, via the fundamental theorem, gets a composition series in which every quotient is cyclic — hence Abelian — and the group is what Part 2 called solvable.

Theorem · Galois (exact, char 0)
A polynomial $f \in \mathbb{Q}[X]$ is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q})$ (where $E$ is the splitting field of $f$) is a solvable group.

And now the punchline. In Part 2, Figure 7, we planted $A_5$: the smallest non-Abelian simple group. Because it is simple, its only composition series is $\{e\} \triangleleft A_5$, whose sole factor is $A_5$ itself — which is not Abelian. Therefore $A_5$ is not solvable. Therefore any polynomial whose Galois group is $A_5$, or whose Galois group contains $A_5$ as a composition factor (such as $\mathrm{Sym}_5$), has no radical formula for its roots.

The polynomial $x^5 - 6x + 3 \in \mathbb{Q}[X]$ has Galois group $\mathrm{Sym}_5$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. (This is a well-known example — irreducible by Eisenstein with $p = 3$, has exactly three real and two complex roots, which is enough to force the group to be all of $\mathrm{Sym}_5$.) The composition series of $\mathrm{Sym}_5$ is $\{e\} \triangleleft A_5 \triangleleft \mathrm{Sym}_5$, with factors $A_5$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2$. One of those factors is not Abelian. The chain breaks.

Theorem · Abel–Ruffini (exact)
There is no general formula in radicals for the roots of the general polynomial of degree 5 or higher. Concretely: there exists a polynomial $f(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ of degree 5 whose roots cannot be expressed using $+, -, \times, \div$ and $n$th roots starting from rational numbers.

The reason is not a technicality; it is a structural fact about groups. Any tower of radical extensions produces a solvable Galois group, because each cyclic step adds an Abelian composition factor. To break out of solvability, the Galois group of the whole polynomial would need to contain a non-Abelian simple factor. Below $\mathrm{Sym}_5$ that is impossible; at $\mathrm{Sym}_5$ it becomes not only possible but generic. This is the boundary the number 5 crosses.

Where the picture lies
The equivalence "solvable by radicals ⇔ solvable Galois group" is exact in characteristic 0, where all irreducibles are separable. In characteristic $p$, additional care is needed (Paulin's separability caveat). Also: some individual quintics are solvable by radicals ($x^5 - 1$, for instance, has cyclic Galois group $\mathbb{Z}/4$). Abel–Ruffini says the general quintic is not, and gives a specific counterexample.

§7 All five threads, closed

We began with a machine combining two things into a third. Twenty-something figures later, the same machine has decided the fate of the quintic. It is worth pausing to see how each of the five threads that opened Part 1 ends.

  • The operation table became the Cayley table (Part 2), then the twin tables of a ring (Part 3), and finally the Cayley table of the Galois group — a permutation group acting on a finite set of roots (Figs 4, 5). The rules of the table decided everything.
  • The clock — $\mathbb{Z}/n$, whose primes are units and whose composites are zero-divisors — reappeared as the roots of unity $\zeta_n$, generating cyclotomic fields whose Galois groups are $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$. Roots of $x^n - 1$ are literally the ticks of the clock, lifted into $\mathbb{C}$.
  • The atoms came in three flavors — primes of $\mathbb{Z}$, simple groups, irreducible polynomials — and Part 4 chained them together. The atom-of-a-ring (an irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$) creates a field extension; the atom-of-a-group (a simple factor in the Galois group) decides whether that field is reachable by radicals. Two atomic zoos, one bridge: the fundamental theorem of Galois theory. And $A_5$, the smallest non-Abelian simple atom, is the specific brick that cannot be climbed by radicals.
  • The partition-by-coloring language — equivalence classes, then cosets, then ideal-cosets — became the intermediate fields of the Galois correspondence (Fig 5). Coloring subgroups by conjugacy class colors the corresponding subfields by whether $K/F$ is normal.
  • The structure-preserving maps — bijections in Part 1, homomorphisms in Part 2, ring homomorphisms in Part 3 — closed as field automorphisms in Part 4. And the shape of the First Isomorphism Theorem, met three times (Parts 2, 3, and inside Galois's third statement above: $\mathrm{Gal}(K/F) \cong G / \mathrm{Gal}(E/K)$), has done every last piece of work. It is the same theorem, four times.

There is a way to say all of this in one sentence, which we have avoided saying until now because it is a slogan the shape of the whole subject: algebra studies objects together with the maps that preserve their structure. Sets and functions; groups and homomorphisms; rings and ring homomorphisms; fields and field automorphisms. Four instances of one idea. The reason Galois theory works — the reason a single group can encode the arithmetic of an entire field — is that the fourth instance turned inward: field automorphisms are the group. In each of the earlier instances, the object and the maps were separate. In Galois theory they are the same organism.

The finale, of course, is not really an ending. Every one of the objects in Paulin's Chapter 5 has consequences — for number theory, geometry, topology, cryptography, and physics — that we have not touched. But the machinery you built up in Parts 1–3 was designed to answer, in this fourth movement, a question posed in 1799 and only fully understood in 1832 by a nineteen-year-old writing in a Paris jail cell the night before he died in a duel. That the machinery worked at all — that a composition series of a finite group could decide whether a polynomial has a root formula — remains, three centuries after Cardano, one of the most beautiful pieces of reasoning in mathematics.
