Composition, Sets, and the Structure of ℤ

Algebra begins with a single act: taking two things and producing a third. Everything that follows — groups, rings, fields, Galois theory — is a variation on what that act is allowed to look like, and what structure the rule imposes on the things it touches.

This is Part 1 of a four-part series companion to Alexander Paulin's Introduction to Abstract Algebra (Math 113). We assume you are comfortable with real analysis and linear algebra, but have never seen the words group or ring. By the end of this part you will have touched every primitive we need: composition, functions, partitions, the integers, primes, and the clock arithmetic $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ that will recur in every subsequent part.

1. What is algebra?

Real analysis studies the continuous: limits, convergence, the fine structure of the real line. Algebra studies something more skeletal. It asks: given a collection of things and a rule for combining them, what structure is forced upon the collection by the rule alone? The rule is the protagonist; the things it acts on are the stage.

The rule need not be addition or multiplication — it need not even involve numbers. What matters is that it takes two inputs and returns a third, and that we can ask what happens when we chain those operations together. This act — two in, one out — is called composition, and it is the single primitive from which all of algebra grows.

Definition — Binary Operation

A binary operation on a set $S$ is a function $\star : S \times S \to S$. It takes an ordered pair $(a, b)$ and returns an element $a \star b \in S$. The closure requirement — that the output lands back in $S$ — is part of the definition: you cannot fall off the table.

Before we formalise anything, play with the machine below. Set the two inputs, choose the operation, and watch the output. Then look at the number line underneath: it shows the same act of composition, instantiated concretely as motion along $\mathbb{Z}$. The abstract slot-filling on top and the familiar addition below are meant to be visibly the same gesture.

Notice that…

When you switch to $\times$, the number line shows $b$ hops of size $a$. Multiplication is composition — it is addition composed with itself, packaged into a single operation. This nesting of one composition inside another is the first hint of the layering that will define rings in Part 3.

Three properties of an operation $\star$ will matter to us throughout the series. We state them now so you can feel them in your hands before you see them formalised:

  • Associativity: $(a \star b) \star c = a \star (b \star c)$. The machine can be chained in any grouping and the result is the same. Both $+$ and $\times$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ have this; subtraction does not.
  • Commutativity: $a \star b = b \star a$. The order of inputs does not matter. Addition and multiplication on $\mathbb{Z}$ commute; matrix multiplication does not.
  • Identity: there exists $e \in S$ with $e \star a = a \star e = a$ for all $a$. For $+$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ this is $0$; for $\times$ it is $1$. The identity is the element that does nothing.

We will not study these in isolation for long. The power of algebra comes from demanding some of these properties and watching what the remaining freedom allows — or forbids.

2. Sets and functions

The combining machine needs things to combine. In algebra the things live in a set, and the way sets talk to each other is through functions. A function $f : S \to T$ is a total, deterministic assignment: every element of $S$ gets exactly one partner in $T$. But not all assignments are created equal. Three flavours will recur under different names in every part of this series, so we pin them down now with something you can touch.

Definition — Injective, Surjective, Bijective

Let $f : S \to T$.

Injective (one-to-one): no two elements of $S$ share a target. Formally, $f(a) = f(b) \Rightarrow a = b$.

Surjective (onto): every element of $T$ is hit. Formally, $\forall\, t \in T,\; \exists\, s \in S : f(s) = t$.

Bijective: both injective and surjective. A bijection establishes a perfect matching and admits an inverse $f^{-1} : T \to S$.

Notice that…

If $|S| < |T|$, no function can be surjective (pigeonhole). If $|S| > |T|$, no function can be injective. A bijection is possible only when $|S| = |T|$, and even then it requires a careful matching — try scrambling the arrows and watch the badges flip. This is the finite shadow of a fact that will return in Part 2 as Cayley's theorem: every group is a subgroup of a permutation group, i.e. a collection of bijections.

Two sets that admit a bijection between them are, for all structural purposes, the same set with relabelled elements. We write $S \cong T$ and say they are equinumerous. This is the first appearance of the categorical spine that will run through the series: we rarely care what things are; we care how they relate, and the structure-preserving maps between them.

3. Equivalence relations and partitions

Sometimes we want to stop distinguishing things that "behave the same" for our purposes. The integers $3$ and $7$ are different numbers, but on a clock with $4$ hours they land in the same position. Before we can build that clock, we need the general machinery for declaring two things equal — and the remarkable fact that doing so is the same thing as cutting the set into disjoint blocks.

Definition — Equivalence Relation

A relation $\sim$ on a set $S$ is an equivalence relation if it is:

Reflexive: $a \sim a$ for all $a \in S$.
Symmetric: $a \sim b \Rightarrow b \sim a$.
Transitive: $a \sim b,\; b \sim c \Rightarrow a \sim c$.

The equivalence class of $a$ is $[a] = \{b \in S : b \sim a\}$. The set of all classes is written $S/\!\sim$.

Where the picture lies

The equivalence-relation $\Leftrightarrow$ partition correspondence is exact — it is a theorem, not a metaphor. Given any equivalence relation, the classes form a genuine partition (disjoint, covering); given any partition, "is in the same block as" is a genuine equivalence relation. The animation of dots sliding into stacks is just decoration on top of an isomorphism of concepts. What the picture does not tell you is which relation to choose — that is always imposed from outside, by the problem at hand.

Notice that…

When $m = 4$, the set $\{0,1,\dots,11\}$ splits into exactly $4$ blocks: $\{0,4,8\}$, $\{1,5,9\}$, $\{2,6,10\}$, $\{3,7,11\}$. These blocks are the elements of $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. We have just constructed our first quotient structure — though we will not name it that until Part 2. For now, hold the image: a partition is what you get when you quotient by a relation.

4. The integers under addition

With the primitives of composition, functions, and partitions in hand, we turn to the first structure Paulin studies in detail: the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ equipped with $+$ and $\times$. These are not arbitrary operations — they satisfy a tight set of axioms, and the interplay between those axioms is what makes number theory possible.

Start with addition alone. The integers under $+$ form what Part 2 will call a group: the operation is associative, there is an identity ($0$), and every element $n$ has an inverse ($-n$). But $\mathbb{Z}$ has a more special property still: it is singly generated. Pick up the element $1$ and compose it with itself repeatedly, and you reach every integer — forward through the positives, backward (using the inverse) through the negatives. The whole infinite set unfolds from a single seed.

Figure 4 — $\mathbb{Z}$ generated by $1$. Press $+1$ repeatedly to walk right; $-1$ to walk left. The ghost trail records where you have been. Starting from $0$, nothing is out of reach: every integer is a finite composition of $1$'s and $-1$'s. This is the seed of "cyclic" — the property that will define the simplest groups in Part 2.

Theorem — Well-ordering of ℕ

Every non-empty subset of $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ has a least element. This is the keystone of integer induction: it lets us prove statements about all of $\mathbb{Z}$ by climbing up from $0$, and it underwrites the division algorithm, Euclid's algorithm, and unique factorisation. You have met it in real analysis; we will lean on it heavily here.

What about multiplication? The integers under $\times$ are not a group: $2$ has no multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb{Z}$. But $\times$ interacts with $+$ through distributivity — $a(b+c) = ab + ac$ — and it is this interaction, not either operation alone, that gives $\mathbb{Z}$ its ring structure (the subject of Part 3). For now, we use $+$ and $\times$ together to ask the question that motivates the rest of this part: how do integers factor?

5. Primes and unique factorization

An integer $n > 1$ is prime if its only positive divisors are $1$ and itself. Otherwise it is composite. The primes are the multiplicative atoms of $\mathbb{Z}$: every integer $> 1$ can be broken into them, and — this is the deep fact — the decomposition is unique up to reordering.

Theorem — Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (FTA)

Every integer $n > 1$ can be written as a product of primes: $$n = p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k}.$$ This factorisation is unique up to the order of the factors. That is, if $n = q_1^{f_1} \cdots q_l^{f_l}$ is another such product, then $k = l$ and, after reordering, $p_i = q_i$ and $e_i = f_i$ for all $i$.

The existence of a factorisation is easy (keep dividing). The uniqueness is the hard part, and it rests on a property of primes that does not look obvious until you need it: if $p$ is prime and $p \mid ab$, then $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$. We call this Euclid's lemma, and it is equivalent to FTA. In Part 3 we will see that this property — "irreducible implies prime" — is what distinguishes the nice rings (UFDs) from the wild ones.

This is the first appearance of the atoms-and-molecules thread that will run through all four parts: in Part 2 it returns as composition series and Jordan–Hölder; in Part 4 it returns as the reason the quintic is unsolvable.

Two quick but load-bearing consequences of FTA, both of which we will need on the other side:

  • Infinitude of primes. For any finite list $p_1, \dots, p_k$, the number $N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1$ is not divisible by any $p_i$ (it leaves remainder $1$). So $N$ has a prime factor not on the list. The primes are infinite — Euclid's proof, unchanged in 2300 years.
  • GCD as a minimum. The gcd of $a$ and $b$ is the product of each prime raised to the smaller of its two exponents. This makes $\gcd(a,b)$ visible in the atom bin, and it is the bridge to the Euclidean algorithm in Part 3.

6. Congruences: ℤ/mℤ

Everything in this part converges on one object. Take the integers, declare two of them equivalent when they differ by a multiple of $m$, and quotient by that relation. The result is a finite set of $m$ equivalence classes — the integers modulo $m$, written $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$. On this set, addition and multiplication descend from $\mathbb{Z}$, and the question of which arithmetic properties survive turns out to hinge entirely on whether $m$ is prime.

Definition — Congruence mod m

For $m \geq 1$, define $a \equiv b \pmod{m}$ if $m \mid (a - b)$. This is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{Z}$; its classes are $[a] = a + m\mathbb{Z}$. The set of classes is $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} = \{[0], [1], \dots, [m-1]\}$. Addition and multiplication are defined by $[a] + [b] = [a+b]$ and $[a] \cdot [b] = [ab]$, and one checks these are well-defined — the answer does not depend on which representative you pick.

That last sentence — well-defined — is doing heavy lifting. It says: even though each class has infinitely many representatives ($[3] = [7] = [11] = \dots$ when $m = 4$), the sum and product of classes depend only on the classes themselves, not on the representatives chosen. You can verify this directly: if $a \equiv a'$ and $b \equiv b' \pmod{m}$, then $a + b \equiv a' + b' \pmod{m}$, and similarly for multiplication. The clock below makes this visceral.

Where the picture lies

The clock captures addition and multiplication in $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ exactly — the wrap-around is not a metaphor, it is the modular arithmetic. What the picture does not capture is the underlying quotient structure: the dial shows the classes $[0], \dots, [m{-}1]$ but hides the infinitely many representatives inside each one. The colouring (green $=$ unit, red $=$ zero-divisor) is also exact, by definition of gcd. The only thing the round shape "suggests" that is not literally true is a metric — the classes are not spatially equidistant in any algebraically meaningful way; the circle is just a convenient way to draw a cyclic list.

Definition — Zero-divisors and Units in ℤ/mℤ

In $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$:

Zero-divisor: a nonzero class $[a]$ such that $[a] \cdot [b] = [0]$ for some nonzero $[b]$. This happens exactly when $\gcd(a, m) > 1$.

Unit: a class $[a]$ that has a multiplicative inverse — i.e. there exists $[b]$ with $[a][b] = [1]$. This happens exactly when $\gcd(a, m) = 1$. The set of units is written $(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^\times$.

Notice that…

Slide $m$ to a prime — say $7$ — and watch every nonzero tick turn green. There are no red ticks, because no integer between $1$ and $6$ shares a factor with $7$. This means every nonzero element is a unit, which means every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. That is the definition of a field. Slide $m$ to a composite — say $6$ — and red ticks appear: $[2] \cdot [3] = [0]$, so $[2]$ and $[3]$ are zero-divisors, and inverses for them cannot exist. The distinction between "prime" and "composite" for $m$ is, algebraically, the distinction between "field" and "not field."

Theorem — ℤ/pℤ is a field ⇔ p is prime

For $m \geq 2$, the ring $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ is a field if and only if $m$ is prime. When $m$ is prime, every nonzero class is a unit (by Euclid's lemma: $\gcd(a,p) = 1$ for $1 \leq a < p$), so inverses exist and the ring is a field. When $m$ is composite, say $m = ab$ with $1 < a, b < m$, then $[a][b] = [0]$ with both factors nonzero, so zero-divisors exist and the ring cannot be a field.

This is the hinge on which the whole series turns. The clock $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ is our first example of a quotient structure: we started with something infinite ($\mathbb{Z}$), imposed an equivalence relation, and obtained something finite whose arithmetic we can hold in our hands. In Part 2 the same construction — quotient by a subgroup — will produce quotient groups. In Part 3, quotient by an ideal will produce quotient rings, and the theorem above will generalise to "$R/I$ is a field $\Leftrightarrow$ $I$ is maximal." In Part 4, field extensions are quotients of polynomial rings, and the Galois group will be the group of symmetries that respect the quotient.

The recurring threads are now all in play. The clock will return as the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ in Part 2, as the ring $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ in Part 3, and as the roots of unity in $\mathbb{C}$ in Part 4. The operation table beside the dial — our first Cayley table — will be the central object of Part 2. The colouring by partition will become coset-colouring, then ideal-coset-colouring, then the lattice of intermediate fields. And the atoms in the factorisation bin will return as simple groups, then irreducible polynomials, then — at the very end — as the non-abelian simple group $A_5$ that makes the quintic unsolvable.
