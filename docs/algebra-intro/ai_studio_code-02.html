Part 2: Groups

Companion to Paulin's Introduction to Abstract Algebra

In Part 1, we saw that the structure of ℤ and the clock ℤ/mℤ share fundamental properties regarding how their elements compose. Groups are the abstract encapsulation of these properties. We drop the numbers entirely and keep only the rules of composition.

3.1 Basic Definitions

Fundamental Definition
A group is a set G, together with a binary operation ✱, such that:
1. (Associativity): (a ✱ b) ✱ c = a ✱ (b ✱ c)
2. (Identity): ∃ e ∈ G such that a ✱ e = e ✱ a = a
3. (Inverses): Given a, ∃ b such that a ✱ b = b ✱ a = e

We can visualize the entirety of a finite group by drawing its Cayley table: a grid where the row is the first element, the column is the second, and the cell is their product. The three axioms dictate the geometry of this grid.

3.2 Subgroups, Cosets and Lagrange's Theorem

A subgroup H is a subset of G that is itself a group. We can use H to define an equivalence relation on G: two elements are related if you can multiply one by an element of H to get the other. The equivalence classes of this relation are called left cosets, written gH.

Lagrange's Theorem
Let G be a finite group and H ⊂ G a subgroup. Then the size of H divides the size of G.

3.5 The Orbit-Stabiliser Theorem

When a group G acts on a set S, the elements of S are moved around. For a specific item s ∈ S, the orbit is everywhere it can possibly be moved to. The stabiliser is the subgroup of G that leaves s perfectly in place.

3.8 Normal Subgroups and Isomorphism Theorems

A homomorphism φ : G → H is a map that preserves the group structure. The kernel of φ is the set of elements in G that map to the identity in H. The kernel is always a normal subgroup, which allows us to cleanly collapse the group into a quotient.

The First Isomorphism Theorem
Let φ : G → H be a homomorphism. Then the induced map from the quotient group G/\ker(φ) to the image Im(φ) is an isomorphism.

3.12 The Classification of Finite Groups

Finite group theory is much like the theory of chemical molecules. The simple groups (groups with no normal subgroups other than trivial ones) act as atoms. Every finite group can be broken down into a composition series—a stack of these simple atoms.
