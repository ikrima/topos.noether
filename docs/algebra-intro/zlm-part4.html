Field Extensions & Galois Theory

A field is a ring in which every nonzero element is a unit — you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide. The rational numbers, the reals, the complex numbers, and the finite fields $\mathbb{F}_p$ are all fields. But fields can be larger or smaller than each other: $\mathbb{Q}$ sits inside $\mathbb{R}$, which sits inside $\mathbb{C}$. A field extension $E/F$ is the study of one field living inside another, and the central question — how much bigger is $E$, and what symmetries does it admit over $F$? — is answered by the Galois group. This is the apex of the series: every thread we have woven returns here, and the payoff is a theorem that explains why the quintic cannot be solved by radicals.

This is Part 4 of the series companion to Paulin's Introduction to Abstract Algebra, covering Chapter 5. The clock returns as roots of unity in $\mathbb{C}$. The atoms from Part 2's composition series return as the obstruction to solvability. The coloring-by-partition returns as the lattice of intermediate fields. And the structure-preserving maps — bijections in Part 1, homomorphisms in Part 2, ring homomorphisms in Part 3 — become field automorphisms: symmetries of a field extension that fix the base field.

1. An extension is a new dimension

If $F$ is a field and $E \supseteq F$ is a larger field, then $E$ is naturally a vector space over $F$: you can add elements of $E$ and multiply them by scalars from $F$, and all the vector-space axioms hold. This is the bridge to your linear-algebra intuition, and it is the single most important observation in the theory. The degree $[E : F]$ of the extension is the dimension of $E$ as an $F$-vector space. A finite-degree extension is called algebraic (every element satisfies a polynomial over $F$); an infinite-degree extension is transcendental.

The simplest nontrivial example is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$: the smallest field containing $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\sqrt{2}$. As a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space, it has basis $\{1, \sqrt{2}\}$, so every element has the form $a + b\sqrt{2}$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$. The degree is $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) : \mathbb{Q}] = 2$. Multiplication by a fixed element $c + d\sqrt{2}$ is a linear map on this 2D space — it is given by a $2 \times 2$ matrix. This is the linear-algebra muscle you already have, applied to fields.

Notice that…

The matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} c & 2d \\ d & c \end{pmatrix}$. The off-diagonal $2d$ comes from $\sqrt{2}^2 = 2$ — the minimal polynomial of $\sqrt{2}$ is $X^2 - 2$, and the "$2$" is the constant term of that polynomial, baked into the multiplication table. Every algebraic extension works this way: the minimal polynomial of the adjoined element determines the matrix of multiplication. The determinant $c^2 - 2d^2$ is the norm $N(c + d\sqrt{2})$, and it is nonzero exactly when $c + d\sqrt{2} \neq 0$ (since $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational) — which is why $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ is a field, not just a ring.

Where the picture lies

The 2D plane picture is exact for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ — it really is a 2-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space, and multiplication really is a linear map given by that matrix. What the picture does not capture is higher-degree extensions: $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ has degree $6$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ (a 6-dimensional space we cannot draw). The 2D intuition scales algebraically — every finite extension is a finite-dimensional vector space — but not visually. 2. The minimal polynomial as a cage

If $\alpha$ is algebraic over $F$, the set $I_\alpha = \{f \in F[X] : f(\alpha) = 0\}$ is a nonzero ideal of $F[X]$ (nonzero because $\alpha$ is algebraic). Since $F[X]$ is a PID (Part 3), $I_\alpha = (p_\alpha)$ for a unique monic polynomial $p_\alpha$, called the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $F$. It is irreducible (else $\alpha$ would satisfy a smaller polynomial), and it is the unique monic irreducible polynomial vanishing at $\alpha$. The degree of $p_\alpha$ equals $[\mathbb{F}(\alpha) : F]$.

The minimal polynomial is a cage: it tells you that the powers $1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \dots$ cannot all be independent. Once you reach $\alpha^n$ (where $n = \deg p_\alpha$), the polynomial $p_\alpha$ gives a linear relation $\alpha^n = -(\text{lower terms})$, and $\alpha^n$ collapses into a combination of $1, \alpha, \dots, \alpha^{n-1}$. This is Paulin's spanning argument: the first $n$ powers span $F(\alpha)$, and irreducibility makes them linearly independent, so they form a basis.

Theorem — Minimal Polynomial and Degree

Let $\alpha$ be algebraic over $F$ with minimal polynomial $p_\alpha \in F[X]$, monic and irreducible. Then:

$F(\alpha) = F[\alpha]$ — the field generated by $\alpha$ equals the ring generated by $\alpha$ (every nonzero element is a unit because $p_\alpha$ is irreducible, so $F[X]/(p_\alpha)$ is a field).
$[F(\alpha) : F] = \deg p_\alpha$, with basis $\{1, \alpha, \dots, \alpha^{n-1}\}$.
The minimal polynomial is unique (monic) and irreducible, and $p_\alpha$ divides any polynomial vanishing at $\alpha$.

Notice that…

For $\sqrt[3]{2}$, the tower reaches $\alpha^3$ before collapsing. The basis is $\{1, \sqrt[3]{2}, \sqrt[3]{4}\}$ — three independent elements, so $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) : \mathbb{Q}] = 3$. But $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ is not the splitting field of $X^3 - 2$: the other two roots ($\omega\sqrt[3]{2}$ and $\omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}$) are complex, and they are not in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. To split $X^3 - 2$, we must adjoin $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$ as well, giving $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ of degree $6$. This distinction — between a field generated by one root and the field containing all roots — is the difference between a general extension and a normal one, which we develop next.

3. Splitting fields and roots in the complex plane

A polynomial $f \in F[X]$ splits over $E$ if it factors into linear factors in $E[X]$. The splitting field of $f$ over $F$ is the smallest extension of $F$ over which $f$ splits — equivalently, $F$ adjoined with all roots of $f$. Splitting fields exist (by repeatedly adjoining roots) and are unique up to isomorphism. An extension $E/F$ is normal if every irreducible polynomial over $F$ that has a root in $E$ splits completely in $E$; splitting fields are the canonical examples. An extension that is normal and separable (minimal polynomials have distinct roots) is called Galois.

Over $\mathbb{C}$, every polynomial splits (the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra), so we can always draw the roots of a polynomial as points in the complex plane. So $[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5) : \mathbb{Q}] = 4$, and the Galois group is $(\mathbb{Z}/5)^\times \cong \mathbb{Z}/4$ — the units of the clock, returning from Part 1. The five threads converge here: the clock, the roots, the minimal polynomial, the degree, and (in the next section) the symmetries.

4. The Galois group permutes the roots

Let $E/F$ be a field extension. An $F$-automorphism of $E$ is a ring isomorphism $\sigma : E \to E$ that fixes $F$ pointwise ($\sigma(a) = a$ for all $a \in F$). The set of all $F$-automorphisms forms a group under composition — the Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$. If $E$ is the splitting field of a polynomial $f \in F[X]$ with roots $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$, then every $\sigma \in \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ permutes the roots (because $\sigma(f(\alpha_i)) = f(\sigma(\alpha_i)) = 0$, so $\sigma(\alpha_i)$ is also a root). This embeds $\mathrm{Gal}(E/F) \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Sym}_n$ — the Galois group is a subgroup of the permutations of the roots.

But not every permutation is allowed. An automorphism must preserve every algebraic relation among the roots that holds over $F$. If $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = \alpha_3$ (a rational relation), then $\sigma(\alpha_1) + \sigma(\alpha_2) = \sigma(\alpha_3)$. The Galois group is the subgroup of $\mathrm{Sym}_n$ consisting of permutations that respect all such relations. In general, this is much smaller than $\mathrm{Sym}_n$. This is the soul of the subject, and the figure below is its showpiece.

Notice that…

For $(X^2-2)(X^2-3)$, the four roots are $\{\sqrt{2}, -\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, -\sqrt{3}\}$. The key relation is "$r^2 \in \{2, 3\}$" — specifically, $\sqrt{2}^2 = 2$ and $\sqrt{3}^2 = 3$. A permutation that sends $\sqrt{2} \mapsto \sqrt{3}$ would make $\sigma(\sqrt{2})^2 = 3 \neq 2 = \sigma(2)$, breaking the relation. So such a permutation is not in the Galois group. The only allowed permutations are: flip the sign of $\sqrt{2}$ (or not), and independently flip the sign of $\sqrt{3}$ (or not) — four automorphisms, forming $\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$. This is the sense in which $\mathrm{Gal}$ is "the symmetries of the roots that respect all $F$-relations" — and it is generally much smaller than the full symmetric group.

Where the picture lies

The permutation-of-roots picture is exact — the Galois group genuinely embeds in $\mathrm{Sym}_n$, and the image is exactly the relation-preserving permutations (this is a theorem, true for Galois extensions). What the figure does not show is the field structure of $E$: the roots are drawn as points in $\mathbb{C}$, but the automorphisms are field maps, not just point permutations — they must preserve $+$ and $\times$ on all of $E$, not just the roots. The embedding $\mathrm{Gal} \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Sym}_n$ is faithful (injective) for splitting fields, so no information is lost, but the figure elides the full field structure for legibility. The "relations as edges" is a simplification — in general, the relations are all polynomial equations, not just pairwise constraints.

Theorem — |Gal(E/F)| = [E : F] for Galois extensions

If $E/F$ is a finite Galois extension (normal and separable), then $|\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)| = [E : F]$. For splitting fields of separable polynomials in characteristic $0$ (our setting), this always holds. The Galois group has as many elements as the degree of the extension — a remarkable equality that connects the symmetries of $E$ to its size as an $F$-vector space.

5. The Galois correspondence

The deepest theorem of the subject is the Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory. It sets up a bijection between the intermediate fields $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$ and the subgroups of $\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$, and this bijection reverses inclusions: larger fields correspond to smaller subgroups. The correspondence also identifies normal subextensions (those $K/F$ that are themselves Galois) with normal subgroups — closing the loop with Part 2's group theory.

Theorem — Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory

Let $E/F$ be a finite Galois extension with $G = \mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$. There is a bijection:

$$\{\text{intermediate fields } K, \; F \subseteq K \subseteq E\} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \{\text{subgroups } H \leq G\}$$

given by $K \mapsto \mathrm{Gal}(E/K) = \{\sigma \in G : \sigma|_K = \mathrm{id}\}$ and $H \mapsto E^H = \{a \in E : \sigma(a) = a \;\forall \sigma \in H\}$ (the fixed field of $H$).

The bijection is inclusion-reversing: $K_1 \subseteq K_2 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{Gal}(E/K_1) \supseteq \mathrm{Gal}(E/K_2)$. Moreover, $[K : F] = [G : \mathrm{Gal}(E/K)]$ and $[E : K] = |\mathrm{Gal}(E/K)|$. Finally, $K/F$ is Galois (normal) $\Leftrightarrow$ $\mathrm{Gal}(E/K) \trianglelefteq G$, and in that case $\mathrm{Gal}(K/F) \cong G / \mathrm{Gal}(E/K)$.

The subgroup lattice of $\mathrm{Sym}_3$ — which you met in Part 2 — appears here on the right, unchanged. The intermediate fields of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)/\mathbb{Q}$ mirror it exactly. The top field $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ corresponds to the trivial subgroup $\{e\}$ (everything fixes $E$). The bottom field $\mathbb{Q}$ corresponds to the whole group $\mathrm{Sym}_3$ (only the identity fixes all of $\mathbb{Q}$... no — every automorphism fixes $\mathbb{Q}$, so $\mathrm{Gal}(E/\mathbb{Q}) = \mathrm{Sym}_3$ is the whole group). The middle field $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ — a normal (Galois) subextension of degree $2$ — corresponds to $\mathrm{Alt}_3 \trianglelefteq \mathrm{Sym}_3$, a normal subgroup. This is the inclusion-reversing mirror in action.

Where the picture lies

The Galois correspondence is exact — the bijection between intermediate fields and subgroups is a theorem, the inclusion-reversal is exact, and the normal-subextension $\Leftrightarrow$ normal-subgroup correspondence is exact. What the figure does not capture is the quantitative side: $[K:F] = [G:H]$ and $[E:K] = |H|$ are not labeled on every edge (only implied by position). The Hasse diagrams are drawn with fields increasing upward and subgroups increasing downward, to make the inclusion-reversal visible — this is a convention, not a theorem about the lattice's geometry.

6. Why five is different

We arrive at the theorem the series has been building toward. A polynomial equation is solvable by radicals if its roots can be expressed from its coefficients using only $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$, and $n$-th roots (radicals). The quadratic formula (degree 2), Cardano's formula (degree 3), and Ferrari's formula (degree 4) all exist — every polynomial of degree $\leq 4$ is solvable by radicals. But there is no formula for the general degree-5 polynomial. Why?

The answer is Galois's theorem: a polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is a solvable group (Part 2, Section 10). The Galois group of the general degree-$n$ polynomial is $\mathrm{Sym}_n$. For $n = 2, 3, 4$, the group $\mathrm{Sym}_n$ is solvable — its composition factors are all Abelian. But for $n = 5$, $\mathrm{Sym}_5$ contains $A_5$ as a normal subgroup, and $A_5$ is simple and non-Abelian — the atom planted in Part 2. A non-Abelian simple group cannot appear in a solvable composition series, so $\mathrm{Sym}_5$ is not solvable, and the general quintic has no radical formula.

Theorem — Solvable by Radicals ⇔ Solvable Galois Group

Let $f \in F[X]$ with $F$ of characteristic $0$, and let $E$ be its splitting field. Then $f$ is solvable by radicals if and only if $\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)$ is a solvable group — meaning its composition factors are all Abelian (cyclic of prime order).

For the general degree-$n$ polynomial, $\mathrm{Gal} = \mathrm{Sym}_n$. $\mathrm{Sym}_n$ is solvable for $n \leq 4$ (factors: $\mathbb{Z}/2$; $\mathbb{Z}/3, \mathbb{Z}/2$; $\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/3, \mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/2$) but not for $n \geq 5$, because $A_n$ is simple and non-Abelian for $n \geq 5$. Hence no radical formula for the general quintic.

Notice that…

The composition series of $\mathrm{Sym}_4$ is $\{e\} \triangleleft V_4 \triangleleft A_4 \triangleleft \mathrm{Sym}_4$, with factors $\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/3, \mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/2$ — all Abelian, all green. That is why Ferrari's formula exists. The series for $\mathrm{Sym}_5$ is $\{e\} \triangleleft A_5 \triangleleft \mathrm{Sym}_5$, with factors $A_5$ (red — non-Abelian simple) and $\mathbb{Z}/2$. The red block is the obstruction. This is the same $A_5$ you met in Part 2 as "the first non-Abelian atom" — planted then, harvested now. The series began in Part 1 with prime factorisation as atoms; it ends here with simple groups as atoms, and the non-Abelian ones as the reason some equations cannot be solved.

Where the picture lies

The solvability chain and the correspondence are exact in characteristic $0$ (our setting, since $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{C}$). The caveat: in characteristic $p$, one must be careful about separability — irreducible polynomials can have repeated roots, and the Galois group can be smaller than $[E:F]$. Paulin handles this with the separable degree and the purely inseparable case; we state the theorem in its characteristic-$0$ form, as Paulin does for most of Chapter 5. The composition series pictured are the genuine series; $A_5$ is genuinely simple (a theorem of Galois) and genuinely non-Abelian. The "obstruction" is not a metaphor — it is the literal algebraic reason.

Coda

The four-part series is complete. Every thread we planted in Part 1 has been harvested here. The clock from Part 1 — $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ — returned as the cyclic group in Part 2, as the ring $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ in Part 3, and finally as the roots of unity in $\mathbb{C}$: the $n$-th roots of unity are literally the clock, realised as complex numbers on the unit circle, and their Galois group is the group of units $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$. The Cayley table from Part 2 became the operation table of a quotient ring in Part 3 and the implicit structure of the Galois group here. The coloring by partition — equivalence classes in Part 1, cosets and conjugacy classes in Part 2, ideal cosets in Part 3 — became the lattice of intermediate fields, mirrored by the subgroup lattice. The atoms — primes in Part 1, simple groups in Part 2, irreducible polynomials in Part 3 — returned as the composition factors of the Galois group, with the non-Abelian atom $A_5$ as the obstruction to solvability. And the structure-preserving maps — functions in Part 1, group homomorphisms in Part 2, ring homomorphisms in Part 3 — culminated in field automorphisms, the elements of the Galois group itself.

The categorical spine, worn lightly throughout, is now visible: each part studied objects and the structure-preserving maps between them — sets and functions, groups and homomorphisms, rings and ring homomorphisms, fields and automorphisms. The Galois correspondence is the apex of this pattern: it relates two categories (fields and groups) by an inclusion-reversing equivalence, and the impossibility of the quintic is, at bottom, a statement about an object in one category (the group $A_5$) that has no counterpart allowing decomposition in the other.

Galois died at twenty. He wrote down these ideas the night before the duel, in a letter to a friend, scrawled in the margins: "I have not time. I have not time." The theorems survived. Two hundred years later, they still say exactly why the quintic cannot be solved — and why, when you build mathematics from composition upward, you arrive, inevitably, at an atom that does not yield.
