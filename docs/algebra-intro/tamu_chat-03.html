Rings and Fields

A companion to Alexander Paulin's Introduction to Abstract Algebra, Chapter 4.

Part 2 built a world with one operation. Real algebra has two — the integers are the archetype, with $+$ and $\times$ locked together by distributivity. A ring is any set carrying two such operations, and the interaction between them is where all the new phenomena live: zero-divisors, ideals, quotients, polynomials, and — as we will see at the end — a periodic table of algebraic atoms that will decide the fate of the quintic.

§1 Two operations, locked together

Definition · ring
A ring is a set $R$ with two operations $+$ and $\times$ such that:
  1. $(R, +)$ is an Abelian group, with identity $0$.
  2. $(R, \times)$ is associative with an identity $1$.
  3. Multiplication distributes over addition: $a(b+c) = ab + ac$ and $(b+c)a = ba + ca$.
If $\times$ is commutative, we call $R$ a commutative ring. All rings in this essay are commutative.

Two operations, three axiom-groups, one bridge — distributivity. This one law is what makes rings hard: additively, everything is an Abelian group and behaves nicely, but multiplication can misbehave in delicate ways that ripple through addition. To feel distributivity concretely, look at it as the area of a rectangle. Drag the sides.

For $\mathbb{Z}$ the picture is exact: literal unit squares, literal areas. For an abstract ring the picture is metaphorical — but the axiom is what it depicts.

§2 Zero, units, and zero-divisors

In a ring the additive identity $0$ has a distinguishing property: $0 \cdot a = 0$ for every $a$. In an ordinary environment like $\mathbb{Z}$, this is the only way to produce $0$ as a product: if $ab = 0$ then $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. Rings without this cancellation property have a name, and they are everywhere.

Definition · integral domain, unit, zero-divisor
Let $R$ be a nonzero commutative ring.
  • A unit is an element $u \in R$ with a multiplicative inverse: $uv = 1$ for some $v \in R$. The units form a group $R^\times$ under $\times$.
  • A zero-divisor is a nonzero $a \in R$ for which there exists a nonzero $b$ with $ab = 0$.
  • $R$ is an integral domain if it has no zero-divisors. A field is a commutative ring in which every nonzero element is a unit.

Every field is an integral domain (trivially — inverses forbid $ab = 0$ unless $a$ or $b$ is $0$). And a beautiful small fact: every finite integral domain is a field. The clock from Part 1 is the fastest way to see all of this together. Here it is again, now presented as a genuine ring, both operations exposed on their own Cayley tables.

Slide $m$. When $m$ is composite, the $\times$ table is speckled with red — nonzero elements combining to produce $0$. When $m$ is prime, all the red vanishes and every nonzero row contains a $1$: everything is a unit, so we have a field. This is Paulin's foundational example, and every commutative ring you will meet either resembles this or fails to in some specific way.

Theorem (Paulin, exact)
Every finite integral domain is a field. In particular, $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ is a field if and only if $m$ is prime.

§3 Homomorphisms, ideals, and the ring analogue of a normal subgroup

A ring homomorphism is a map $\varphi : R \to S$ preserving both operations and sending $1$ to $1$. As in Part 2, the kernel is a distinguished subset — but it is not merely a subring. It has a stronger closure property.

Definition · ideal
A subset $I \subseteq R$ is an ideal if
  • $I$ is an additive subgroup ($0 \in I$; closed under $+$ and $-$);
  • absorption: for every $r \in R$ and $x \in I$, both $rx$ and $xr$ lie in $I$.
Kernels of ring homomorphisms are always ideals, and every ideal is the kernel of some homomorphism.

Absorption is the ring analogue of normality. An ideal is not just a subring closed under multiplication among its own members — it swallows anything from the ambient ring that touches it. In $\mathbb{Z}$, the multiples of $6$ form an ideal $(6) = \{\ldots,-12,-6,0,6,12,\ldots\}$: multiply any multiple of $6$ by any integer whatsoever, and you land back in the multiples of $6$. That is absorption.

When $R = \mathbb{Z}$, this is nothing exotic: the ideal $(a)$ is just $a\mathbb{Z}$, spaced every $a$ units on the line. In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (the Gaussian integers), the ideal $(a)$ is a lattice inside a lattice, rotated and scaled by the complex number $a$. In both cases the multiplication rule drags the ideal into itself.

Ideals let us build quotients. Just as $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ is $\mathbb{Z}$ modulo the ideal $(m)$, we can form $R/I$ for any ring $R$ and ideal $I$: the elements are the additive cosets $r + I$, and both operations descend well-definedly. And the First Isomorphism Theorem carries over verbatim.

Theorem · First Iso Theorem for rings
For a ring homomorphism $\varphi : R \to S$, $\ker\varphi$ is an ideal, $\mathrm{Im}\,\varphi$ is a subring, and $$ R/\ker\varphi \;\;\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\;\; \mathrm{Im}\,\varphi $$ is an isomorphism of rings. (Compare with Part 2's group version: same shape, twice as much structure to preserve.)

§4 Prime and maximal ideals: reading the quotient

Two special classes of ideal control whether the quotient is nice.

Definition · prime, maximal
A proper ideal $I \subsetneq R$ is
  • prime if $ab \in I \Rightarrow a \in I$ or $b \in I$;
  • maximal if no proper ideal strictly contains $I$.
Theorem (exact)
For a commutative ring $R$ and a proper ideal $I$:
  • $R/I$ is an integral domain $\iff$ $I$ is prime.
  • $R/I$ is a field $\iff$ $I$ is maximal.
  • Since every field is a domain, every maximal ideal is prime (but not conversely).

The picture: pick an ideal $I \subset R$, form $R/I$, and look at its little multiplication table. If the table has no zero-divisors, $I$ is prime. If, in addition, every nonzero element of the table has an inverse, $I$ is maximal.

Notice that in $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, the ideal $(3)$ is maximal (quotient is $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, a field), the ideal $(4)$ is not even prime (quotient is $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, has zero-divisors: $2\cdot 2 = 0$), and the ideal $(2)$ is maximal too. Prime and maximal ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ correspond exactly to the prime divisors of $n$.

§5 Polynomial rings

The other essential ring, alongside $\mathbb{Z}$, is $F[X]$ — polynomials in one variable with coefficients in a field $F$. A polynomial is nothing but a finite tuple of coefficients:

Theorem (exact)
If $R$ is an integral domain, so is $R[X]$, and $\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g$. Consequently: over a field $F$, $F[X]$ is an integral domain in which the units are exactly the nonzero constants.

The degree function is more than a bookkeeping device. It plays the role, for $F[X]$, that $|\cdot|$ plays for $\mathbb{Z}$: both give us a way to run the Euclidean algorithm. That is not a coincidence; it is the definition of the class of rings where the algorithm works.

§6 The Euclidean algorithm, twice

Definition · Euclidean domain, PID, UFD
A commutative domain $R$ is a
  • Euclidean domain if it has a "size" function $N$ with a division algorithm: for any $a,b\in R,\;b\neq 0$, there exist $q,r$ with $a = bq + r$ and either $r = 0$ or $N(r) < N(b)$.
  • Principal ideal domain (PID) if every ideal is generated by a single element.
  • Unique factorization domain (UFD) if every nonzero non-unit factors uniquely (up to units and reordering) as a product of irreducibles.
Euclidean $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD.

The two canonical Euclidean domains are $\mathbb{Z}$ (size = absolute value) and $F[X]$ (size = degree). Watch the same algorithm play out on both, one step at a time.

The polynomials on the right are a preloaded example over $\mathbb{F}_5$; the integers on the left are chosen with the sliders. The point of the parallel is not that anything unexpected happens — quite the opposite. The point is that the same argument, using degree instead of absolute value, proves that $F[X]$ is a Euclidean domain, hence a PID, hence a UFD. Two examples of a single mechanism.

§7 The nesting UFD ⊃ PID ⊃ Euclidean

The three properties above form a chain of ever-weakening niceness. In each layer live examples that don't live in the next. And outside all three, in the wild, live rings where "atom" and "prime" come apart.

Where the picture lies
The inclusions Euclidean $\subset$ PID $\subset$ UFD are exact and strict, and the counterexample $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ genuinely lives outside UFD. The concrete tokens we place are illustrative — many other rings live in each region. And the picture cannot capture the quantitative difference between the layers: a PID has just barely fewer nice properties than a Euclidean domain, but strong enough to derive most classical results.
Definition · irreducible, prime (in a domain)
In an integral domain $R$, a nonzero non-unit $p$ is
  • irreducible if $p = ab$ forces $a$ or $b$ to be a unit;
  • prime if $p \mid ab$ forces $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$.
Prime always implies irreducible. The converse holds precisely in UFDs — that is the defining feature: in a UFD, irreducibles are the atoms, unambiguously.

In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, $2$ and $3$ are irreducible but not prime (each divides $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ without dividing either factor). Two different atomizations of $6$ exist, and neither can be refined into the other. That is what "no unique factorization" looks like in the flesh.

§8 The atoms of $F[X]$: a periodic table per field

For any field $F$, the polynomial ring $F[X]$ is a UFD, and the atoms are the irreducible polynomials. What is astonishing is that the shape of this atomic zoo depends drastically on $F$.

Theorem · Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (referenced)
$\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed: every nonconstant polynomial in $\mathbb{C}[X]$ has a root in $\mathbb{C}$. Equivalently, the only irreducibles in $\mathbb{C}[X]$ are the linear ones. Any $p(X) \in \mathbb{R}[X]$ factors over $\mathbb{R}$ into linears and irreducible quadratics.
Theorem · Eisenstein's criterion
Let $f(X) = a_n X^n + \cdots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$. If there is a prime $p$ dividing $a_0, \ldots, a_{n-1}$ but not $a_n$, and $p^2 \nmid a_0$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

Eisenstein constructs $\mathbb{Q}$-irreducibles of every degree — for instance, $X^n - 2$ satisfies the criterion with $p = 2$ for every $n$. So the atomic zoo of $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ genuinely does have infinitely many irreducibles at every degree, and it is the density and variety of these atoms that will make Part 4 possible.

Where the picture lies
For $\mathbb{C}$, we cannot really draw "all linear atoms" — there are uncountably many, one per complex number. The dot-cloud in the figure samples them. For $\mathbb{Q}$, the sample is honest but tiny: the true zoo is countable but explodes combinatorially with degree.

§9 The threads, layered

Every character introduced in Parts 1 and 2 has now been fitted with a second operation:

  • The operation table is now two operation tables, linked by distributivity (Fig 2, Fig 4). Reading one against the other diagnoses zero-divisors, primes, and maximal ideals.
  • The clock returned intact (Fig 2), enriched with $\times$. Its ideals $(d)$ for $d \mid n$ are the local instance of the general theory.
  • The atoms generalized from primes and simple groups to irreducibles (Fig 8). In a UFD the atomization is unique; in the wild ($\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, Fig 7) it fails.
  • The partition-by-coloring language became ideal cosets (Fig 3, Fig 4): $R$ tiled into copies of $I$; the quotient $R/I$ tiles down to a smaller ring.
  • The structure-preserving maps gained an extra axiom (preserve $\times$ and send $1$ to $1$); kernels are ideals; and the First Isomorphism Theorem repeats, verbatim, at the ring level.

We now have every algebraic ingredient the finale needs. Part 4 puts one specific ring in the spotlight — $F[X]$ over $F = \mathbb{Q}$ — quotients it by an irreducible, watches new fields spring up as extensions, and asks the question that closes the series: which polynomials can be solved by radicals? The atoms of Part 3 will meet the atoms of Part 2, and $A_5$ will have the last word.
