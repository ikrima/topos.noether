Part 2 — Groups

A group is the smallest algebraic machine where composition can be undone. We keep one operation, require it to be associative, ask for a do-nothing element, and demand that every move has a reverse move.

1. The Cayley table, alive

Part 1 ended with operation tables for residues. Now the table becomes the main instrument. A binary operation on a finite set is visible as a square grid: choose a row element, choose a column element, and the cell is their product.

The three group axioms become table geometry. The identity is a row and column that merely copy the headers. Inverses are the places where the identity reappears. Commutativity is mirror symmetry across the diagonal. Associativity is subtler: it compares two different paths through the table.

DefinitionA group is a set G with a binary operation ⋆ such that (a⋆b)⋆c = a⋆(b⋆c), there is an identity e, and every a has an inverse a⁻¹.
Where the picture liesFor a finite group, the Cayley table is exact. Identity, inverse, and commutativity are visually local. Associativity is global: checking one highlighted triple is exact for that triple, not a proof for the whole table.

2. The dihedral playground

Commutative groups feel like clocks: order does not matter. The first useful counterexample is the symmetry group of a regular polygon. Rotate, then reflect; reflect, then rotate. The same two moves generally land in different places.

Theorem-patternThe dihedral group Dₘ has 2m elements: rotations σᵏ and reflections τσᵏ, with ord(σ)=m, ord(τ)=2, and τσ=σ⁻¹τ.
Where the picture liesFor a regular m-gon in the Euclidean plane, this is exact. The drawn polygon is not merely an analogy; its distance-preserving symmetries are the dihedral group.

3. The rotating cube

A symmetry can act on something else. The rotational symmetries of a cube act on its four body diagonals, so every cube rotation secretly writes down a permutation of four colors. This realizes a geometric group as a permutation group.

Paulin connectionThe rotational symmetries of the cube act faithfully on the four pairs of opposite vertices, giving Rot□ ≅ Sym₄.
Where the picture liesThis figure shows rotations only, the orientation-preserving symmetries. The full symmetry group also includes reflections/inversion and has twice as many elements.

4. Cosets tile the group

A subgroup is a closed mini-world inside a group. Pick a subgroup H, then multiply every element of H on the left by g. The result gH is a left coset. Cosets behave like colored tiles: equal-sized, disjoint, and collectively covering the whole group.

Lagrange’s TheoremIf G is finite and H⊂G is a subgroup, then the left cosets of H partition G into equal-sized pieces, so |H| divides |G|.
Where the picture liesThe tiling is exact for finite groups. The visual placement in a grid is decorative; the partition and equal-cardinality claims are the mathematics.

5. Orbit–Stabiliser as conservation

When a group acts on a set, each element of the set has two complementary measurements. The orbit asks: how many places can the point go? The stabiliser asks: how many group elements keep it fixed? The product is conserved.

Orbit–Stabiliser TheoremFor a group G acting on S, if Orb(s) is finite, then (G:Stab(s)) = |Orb(s)|. For finite G, |G| = |Stab(s)|·|Orb(s)|.
Where the picture liesThis is exact for the displayed action on polygon vertices. The bar diagram visualizes cardinalities; it is not additional structure on the group.

6. Homomorphism as collapsing the table

A homomorphism preserves composition. It may forget distinctions, but only in a structured way. Everything that collapses to the identity is the kernel. When the kernel is normal, the collapsed table is well-defined, and the quotient is exactly the image.

First Isomorphism TheoremFor a homomorphism φ:G→H, the induced map G/Ker(φ) → Im(φ), xKer(φ)↦φ(x), is an isomorphism.
Where the picture liesThe collapse is exact for these examples. The drawing shows why normality is needed: every representative in a block must yield the same product block.

7. Atoms & molecules coda

Finite groups can be decomposed by normal-subgroup towers. The quotients are the simple components: group-theoretic atoms. Two molecules can have the same atoms but different structure. That distinction will matter in Part 4 when solvability by radicals meets the non-Abelian atom A₅.

Jordan–Hölder TheoremAny two composition series of a finite group have the same simple quotient factors, up to isomorphism and reordering.
Where the picture liesThe tower metaphor is exact as a diagram of normal subgroup inclusions and quotients. The molecule analogy is suggestive: same atoms do not determine the full group structure.
