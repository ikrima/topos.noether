Part 4: Field Extensions & Galois Theory

Companion to Paulin's Introduction to Abstract Algebra

In Part 3, we saw that polynomial rings over a field F contain "atoms"—irreducible polynomials that cannot be factored further. To find the roots of these polynomials, we must step outside of F into a larger field. This is the heart of Galois Theory: understanding the symmetries of the roots in these new extensions.

5.1 Field Extensions and Minimal Polynomials

Definition
Let E be a field and F ⊂ E a subfield. We call E an extension of F, written E/F. Because E is an Abelian group under addition equipped with scalar multiplication by elements of F, E is naturally an F-vector space.

If we adjoin the root of an irreducible polynomial to ℚ, say α = √2, the new field ℚ(√2) consists of all elements a + b√2. Because every element is a combination of two basis vectors {1, √2}, the dimension—or degree [E:F]—of the extension is 2.

The point v = a + b√2 lives in a 2D vector space over ℚ. Click Multiply by √2 to see the algebraic rule (√2)² = 2 act as a literal linear map, swapping the axes and scaling the 1-axis.

When you repeatedly multiply by α, you generate a sequence of powers: 1, α, α², α³, etc. If α is algebraic, this sequence eventually collapses back into the existing dimensions. The minimal polynomial is the "cage" that forces this collapse.

Watch the sequence of powers αk. When you reach the degree of the extension, the vector hits the "cage" and collapses into a combination of lower dimensions, proving that higher powers cannot escape the finite basis.

5.2 Splitting Fields

A polynomial splits completely when we adjoin all its roots. The splitting field is the smallest extension containing F and every root. For xⁿ - 1, the roots are the roots of unity, and they trace out our familiar clock face in the complex plane.

Roots of xⁿ - 1 plotted in ℂ. The splitting field is generated by throwing all these blue dots into ℚ.

5.3 Galois Theory

We finally arrive at the soul of the subject. Let E be a splitting field. An automorphism of E fixing F is a map that preserves the field structure while leaving the base field totally alone.

Key Insight
Because an automorphism fixes the coefficients in F, it must map roots to roots. Therefore, the Galois Group Gal(E/F) can be thought of as permutations of the roots that preserve all algebraic relationships between them.

Consider the splitting field of (x² - 2)(x² - 3). The roots are ±√2 and ±√3. We can permute these 4 roots in 24 ways, but not all permutations are valid automorphisms.

Valid Automorphism ✓
The solid lines represent the structural relationship "squares to 2" and "squares to 3". A valid Galois permutation must perfectly preserve these edges. Notice what happens when you try to swap a √2 with a √3.

This allows us to draw a perfect dictionary—an inclusion-reversing bijection—between the subfields of E and the subgroups of the Galois Group.

Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory
There is a natural bijection between Intermediate Subfields F ⊂ K ⊂ E and Subgroups H ⊂ Gal(E/F). Furthermore, the larger the subfield, the smaller the subgroup of symmetries fixing it.

5.4 Solving Polynomials By Radicals

For centuries, mathematicians searched for an analogue of the quadratic formula for degree 5 polynomials. Galois proved it is impossible. For a formula to exist, the Galois group must be "solvable"—meaning its composition series consists entirely of Abelian simple components (like the atoms we saw in Part 2).
