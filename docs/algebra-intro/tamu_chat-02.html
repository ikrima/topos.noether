Groups

A companion to Alexander Paulin's Introduction to Abstract Algebra, Chapter 3.

In Part 1 we watched a binary operation combine two elements into a third. That was the raw act. We now demand three modest laws — associativity, an identity, and inverses — and ask what falls out. The answer, remarkably, is almost all the symmetry in mathematics.

§1 The three axioms

Definition · group
A group is a set $G$ with a binary operation $\ast : G \times G \to G$ satisfying:
  1. Associativity: $(a \ast b) \ast c = a \ast (b \ast c)$ for all $a,b,c \in G$.
  2. Identity: there is an element $e \in G$ with $e \ast a = a \ast e = a$ for all $a$.
  3. Inverses: for every $a \in G$ there exists $a^{-1} \in G$ with $a \ast a^{-1} = a^{-1} \ast a = e$.
If additionally $a \ast b = b \ast a$ for all $a,b$, we call $G$ Abelian.

The identity is unique; the inverse of each element is unique. Both facts follow from associativity alone by short algebraic manipulations. Rather than write those manipulations, we can see them, because associativity, identity, and inverses are all readable off a single object: the operation table of Part 1, now rechristened.

Three visible facts. First, the row headed by $e$ copies the column headers verbatim, and the column headed by $e$ copies the row headers — that is the identity axiom, made into geometry. Second, the identity element $e$ appears exactly once in every row and exactly once in every column — that is the inverse axiom: the unique $b$ making $a \ast b = e$ is $a^{-1}$. Third: if the table is symmetric across its main diagonal, the group is Abelian; if not, it is not. Look at ℤ/6 versus Sym₃.

Associativity is the one axiom that does not read off the geometry — you have to check triples. The test associativity toggle picks three random elements and verifies $(a \ast b) \ast c = a \ast (b \ast c)$ so you can watch it happen. Every finite group we will show passes, of course; the point is to feel that this is a real, checkable condition, not a decoration.

Where the picture lies
Each row (and each column) of a Cayley table is a permutation of the elements of $G$ — this is Cayley's theorem visualized, and it is exact. What the table hides is the internal structure of the elements: two groups can have wildly different Cayley tables and yet be isomorphic (permute the row/column labels and one becomes the other). Isomorphism is a fact about the underlying algebra, not about the specific picture.

§2 Where groups come from: symmetry

The Cayley table above is abstract — just element names and combinations. But almost every group you will meet is a group of symmetries of something. The smallest interesting instance is the symmetries of a regular polygon: rotations and reflections that carry the polygon to itself. These form the dihedral group $D_m$, which has $2m$ elements. Here it is with the operation exposed.

Notice that rotations alone form a smaller world inside $D_m$: applying $\sigma$ repeatedly cycles through $m$ configurations before returning to $e$. Reflections take you into the "other half" — the $m$ configurations with reversed orientation. Notice also that $\tau \sigma \neq \sigma \tau$: a reflection followed by a rotation is not the same as the rotation followed by the reflection. Concretely, $\tau \sigma = \sigma^{-1} \tau$, and this single relation, together with $\sigma^m = e$ and $\tau^2 = e$, encodes the entire group.

Definition · order
The order of a group $G$, written $|G|$, is its cardinality. The order of an element $g \in G$ is the smallest positive $n$ with $g^n = e$ (or $\infty$ if no such $n$ exists).

$\sigma$ has order $m$; $\tau$ has order $2$; the group has order $2m$. This is the first non-Abelian group we can genuinely handle — and every non-Abelian phenomenon later in the series is a variant of the trick that dihedral groups are already playing.

Symmetries in three dimensions

The same idea, one dimension up. Consider the cube. Its rotation group has $24$ elements: pick any face to go on top ($6$ choices), then any of $4$ rotations around the vertical axis. But instead of tracking those directly, watch what a rotation does to the four body diagonals. Grab the cube and drag to rotate it in space; the four colored diagonals get permuted, and every permutation of them arises exactly once.

Theorem (exact)
The group of orientation-preserving symmetries of the cube is isomorphic to $\mathrm{Sym}_4$, the symmetric group on four letters. The isomorphism sends each rotation to its action on the four body diagonals.
Where the picture lies
We are showing rotations only — the orientation-preserving symmetries. The full symmetry group of the cube (rotations plus reflections) has $48$ elements and is isomorphic to $\mathrm{Sym}_4 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$, not $\mathrm{Sym}_4$. The cube alone is a subtle object; we are looking at only half of it.

§3 Subgroups, cosets, and Lagrange

Inside every group live smaller groups. A subgroup $H \leq G$ is a subset closed under $\ast$ and inversion — it is a group in its own right, using the inherited operation. Every group has two trivial subgroups: $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself. The interesting ones are between.

Pick a subgroup $H$. Each element $g \in G$ generates a left coset $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$. Distinct cosets are disjoint, and together they exhaust $G$. Watch it happen: pick a group, pick a subgroup, and the entire group tiles into equal-size cosets, one color per coset.

Theorem · Lagrange
If $H$ is a subgroup of a finite group $G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$. In particular, $|G| = [G:H] \cdot |H|$, where $[G:H]$ is the number of distinct cosets of $H$ in $G$.

The proof is exactly the figure: the cosets partition $G$, and each coset has the same size as $H$ (translation by $g$ is a bijection $H \to gH$). Count the pieces. The result is one of the most useful divisibility facts in mathematics — for instance, the order of any element $g \in G$ divides $|G|$, because $\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup.

Where the picture lies (barely)
For an Abelian group, left cosets equal right cosets and there is nothing to worry about. For a non-Abelian group, $gH$ and $Hg$ may differ; the figure shows left cosets. Whether left = right for every $g$ is a special property called normality, and it will be the pivot of the Isomorphism Theorem below.

§4 Group actions, orbits, stabilisers

A group is most vivid when it acts on something. A group action is a map $G \times X \to X$ sending $(g, x) \mapsto g \cdot x$, compatible with the operation ($e \cdot x = x$ and $g \cdot (h \cdot x) = (gh) \cdot x$). Two invariants attach to any point $x$:

  • The orbit $G \cdot x = \{ g \cdot x : g \in G \}$: everywhere the point can reach.
  • The stabiliser $G_x = \{ g \in G : g \cdot x = x \}$: everything that fixes it.

Below, a group acts on a set of tokens. Step through the group's elements; watch the orbit fill in on the left and the stabiliser highlight on the right. The bar at the bottom is a single number: $|G|$. The bar is split into two pieces, $|Orbit|$ and $|Stab|$, whose product is $|G|$. As you change the acting group or the base point, the split moves, but the total does not.

Theorem · Orbit–Stabiliser
For any action of a finite group $G$ on a set $X$ and any $x \in X$, $$|G| = |G \cdot x| \cdot |G_x|.$$ Equivalently, the map $gG_x \mapsto g \cdot x$ is a bijection from left cosets of the stabiliser to points of the orbit.

Two immediate applications. Applied to $G$ acting on itself by conjugation, the orbits are called conjugacy classes, and the theorem becomes the class equation $|G| = \sum_i [G : C_G(x_i)]$, summed over one representative per class. Applied to $G$ acting on the cosets of a subgroup $H$ by left multiplication, it gives an embedding $G \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Sym}_{[G:H]}$ — every finite group is a group of permutations. This is Cayley's theorem, and it is why the symmetric groups $\mathrm{Sym}_n$ are the "universe" of finite groups.

Sylow (stated for reference)
If $|G| = p^a m$ with $p$ prime and $p \nmid m$, then $G$ has subgroups of order $p^a$ (Sylow $p$-subgroups); any two are conjugate; and the number of them is $\equiv 1 \pmod p$ and divides $m$. Sylow's theorems are the deepest general facts we can prove about finite groups from these axioms alone.

§5 Homomorphisms, kernels, quotients

A structure-preserving map between groups is called a homomorphism: a function $\varphi : G \to H$ with $\varphi(a \ast b) = \varphi(a) \ast \varphi(b)$. It automatically sends $e_G$ to $e_H$ and inverses to inverses. A bijective homomorphism is an isomorphism; groups related by one are, algebraically, the same group in different clothes.

Every homomorphism has two attached subgroups: the image $\mathrm{Im}(\varphi) \leq H$ and the kernel $\ker(\varphi) = \{g \in G : \varphi(g) = e_H\} \leq G$. The kernel is not just any subgroup — it is normal, meaning $g K g^{-1} = K$ for all $g$. Normality is precisely the condition that lets us fold the Cayley table.

Theorem · First Isomorphism Theorem
For any group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$, the kernel $K = \ker\varphi$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, the image is a subgroup of $H$, and there is a natural isomorphism $$ G / K \;\;\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\;\; \mathrm{Im}(\varphi). $$

Read the figure. The kernel is the set of $g$ sent to $e_H$; it forms a normal subgroup $K$. The cosets of $K$ each map to a single element of $H$. On the left table, entire blocks of cells (one per coset pair) share the same output color. Collapse each block to a point: the result is the Cayley table on the right. The theorem says this collapse is always possible and always gives an isomorphism onto the image.

Definition · normal subgroup, quotient group
A subgroup $N \leq G$ is normal, written $N \triangleleft G$, if $gN = Ng$ for every $g \in G$ (equivalently $gNg^{-1} = N$). When $N$ is normal, the set of cosets $G/N$ becomes a group under the operation $(gN)(hN) := (gh)N$; this is the quotient group. Normality is exactly the condition making this operation well-defined.

Paulin's Third Isomorphism Theorem says quotients respect nesting: if $K \leq N \leq G$ with both normal in $G$, then $(G/K)/(N/K) \cong G/N$. Geometrically: collapsing in two stages equals collapsing in one. The picture generalizes directly.

§6 Cyclic groups; the classification of finite Abelian groups

The simplest groups of all are the cyclic ones — generated by a single element. In Part 1 we saw ℤ, singly generated under $+$. Folding it modulo $m$ gives $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$, and every finite cyclic group is isomorphic to one of these. Cyclic groups are automatically Abelian, and their Cayley tables are highly regular (each row is the previous row shifted by one).

More is true. Every finite Abelian group breaks up as a direct product of cyclic groups of prime-power order, and the multiset of prime powers is unique. This is the group-theoretic Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic.

Theorem · Structure of Finite Abelian Groups
Every finite Abelian group $A$ is isomorphic to a direct product $$ A \cong \mathbb{Z}/p_1^{a_1}\mathbb{Z} \;\times\; \mathbb{Z}/p_2^{a_2}\mathbb{Z} \;\times\; \cdots \;\times\; \mathbb{Z}/p_k^{a_k}\mathbb{Z}, $$ where the $p_i^{a_i}$ are prime powers, uniquely determined up to reordering.

The proof rhymes exactly with FTA on ℤ. For Abelian groups, the "atoms" are cyclic groups of prime-power order, and the decomposition is unique. For non-Abelian groups the situation is more delicate — we need a new notion of atom.

§7 Atoms and molecules of groups

For general (non-Abelian) groups, the right notion of atom is a simple group: one with no normal subgroups except $\{e\}$ and itself. Simple groups cannot be further quotiented; they are the primes of group theory.

Building up: a composition series of $G$ is a chain of subgroups $$ \{e\} = G_0 \triangleleft G_1 \triangleleft \cdots \triangleleft G_n = G, $$ each normal in the next, whose factors $G_{i+1}/G_i$ are all simple. The stunning fact — Paulin's Jordan–Hölder theorem — is that although the chain itself is not unique, the multiset of simple factors is.

Theorem · Jordan–Hölder
Any two composition series of a finite group $G$ have the same length and the same multiset of composition factors (up to isomorphism and reordering).

Compare and contrast two groups that share atoms but are different molecules. Below, ℤ/6 and Sym₃ are both order-6 groups, both with composition factors $\{\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/3\}$ — chemically identical, structurally different (one Abelian, one not). And planted in the third slot is $A_5$, the alternating group on five letters: the smallest non-Abelian simple group, and — Part 4 will reveal — the reason the quintic has no radical formula.

Definition · solvable
A finite group $G$ is solvable if it has a composition series in which every factor is a cyclic group of prime order (equivalently, every composition factor is Abelian).

Every Abelian group is solvable trivially. $\mathrm{Sym}_3$ and $\mathrm{Sym}_4$ are solvable. But once a composition series is forced to include a non-Abelian simple group as an atom, solvability breaks. The smallest such atom is $A_5$, and it lives inside $\mathrm{Sym}_5$ — a hint of what is coming.

Where the picture lies
The tower-of-blocks visualization is exact: the composition factors are literally what you get by quotienting up the chain, and Jordan–Hölder guarantees the multiset does not depend on the chain. What the picture cannot show is the extension problem — knowing the atoms does not tell you the molecule, as ℤ/6 vs Sym₃ demonstrates.

§8 The threads, again

We have taught our operation to obey axioms. Five things happened, each threading a character introduced in Part 1 into new territory:

  • The operation table became the Cayley table, with axioms as visible geometry (Fig 1, Fig 6). It will return in Part 3 with two operations layered on top.
  • The clock became the general cyclic group, and cosets became a way of tiling any group into equal blocks (Fig 4). In Part 3 the cosets will be of an ideal, and the quotient will be a ring.
  • The atoms generalized: primes became simple groups; unique factorization became Jordan–Hölder (Fig 7). In Part 3 the atoms will be irreducible polynomials; in Part 4, the atom $A_5$ will decide the quintic.
  • The partition-by-coloring language became cosets, orbits, and conjugacy classes (Fig 4, Fig 5). In Part 4 the "colors" will be intermediate fields.
  • The bijection matured into homomorphism (Fig 6). The First Isomorphism Theorem is the shape of the entire subject: kernel–image–quotient. It reappears verbatim for rings, and it is the mechanism by which Galois groups correspond to subfields in Part 4.
