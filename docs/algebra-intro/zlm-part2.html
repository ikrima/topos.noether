Groups

In Part 1 we met composition — two things in, one thing out — and saw it instantiated as addition and multiplication on $\mathbb{Z}$. Now we ask the algebraist's question: what is the minimal set of rules that makes composition behave well, and what structure do those rules force upon the things being composed? The answer is a group, and the surprising fact is how much follows from how little.

This is Part 2 of the series companion to Paulin's Introduction to Abstract Algebra, covering Chapter 3. Every object you touched in Part 1 — the clock, the operation table, the coloring-by-partition — returns here with new structure layered on. The clock becomes the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$. The operation table becomes the Cayley table, the central object of the theory. And the atoms from prime factorization reappear as simple groups, the indecomposable building blocks from which all finite groups are assembled.

1. The three axioms

A group is a set $G$ equipped with a binary operation $\star : G \times G \to G$ satisfying three axioms. You have already met all three in Part 1 as properties of $+$ on $\mathbb{Z}$; the act of abstraction is to demand them and see what follows.

Definition — Group

A group is a pair $(G, \star)$ where $\star : G \times G \to G$ is a binary operation satisfying:

Associativity: $(a \star b) \star c = a \star (b \star c)$ for all $a, b, c \in G$.
Identity: there exists $e \in G$ such that $e \star a = a \star e = a$ for all $a \in G$.
Inverse: for each $a \in G$ there exists $a^{-1} \in G$ such that $a \star a^{-1} = a^{-1} \star a = e$.

If additionally $a \star b = b \star a$ for all $a, b$, the group is Abelian.

Three remarks are in order before we go further. First, closure — that $a \star b \in G$ — is built into the definition of a binary operation ($\star$ maps $G \times G \to G$), so it is not listed as a separate axiom. Second, the identity and inverses are unique: if $e$ and $e'$ both satisfy the identity axiom, then $e = e \star e' = e'$; similarly, if $b$ and $b'$ are both inverses of $a$, then $b = b \star e = b \star (a \star b') = (b \star a) \star b' = e \star b' = b'$. This uniqueness is what lets us speak of the identity and the inverse. Third, the cancellation law holds: $a \star b = a \star c$ implies $b = c$ (multiply on the left by $a^{-1}$), and similarly on the right. This is the group-theoretic shadow of "you can divide both sides of an equation."

You already know many groups. The integers $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ form a group (identity $0$, inverse $-n$). The integers under multiplication do not — $2$ has no multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb{Z}$. But $(\mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\}, \times)$ is a group. From linear algebra: every vector space under addition is an Abelian group, and $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ — the invertible $n \times n$ matrices — is a (non-Abelian) group under matrix multiplication. The operation can be function composition, matrix multiplication, or addition; the axioms don't care.

Where the picture lies

The Cayley table is an exact encoding of a finite group: it lists every product, and the axioms are checkable by inspection (identity = a row matching the header; inverses = identity appearing in each row; associativity = a finite but tedious check). What the table does not reveal at a glance is the group's internal structure — subgroups, normal subgroups, composition factors — those require analysis beyond the raw grid. The associativity detector tests only random triples, not all of them; for the small groups here the check is exhaustive, but in general verifying associativity from a table is $O(|G|^3)$.

3. Cyclic and dihedral groups

Two families of groups will recur throughout the series, and they are best met through direct manipulation. The first is the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ — the clock from Part 1, now recognised as a group under addition. It is generated by a single element: pressing $+1$ repeatedly cycles through every element. Cyclic groups are always Abelian, and every group of prime order is cyclic (we will prove this with Lagrange's theorem below).

The second family is richer. The dihedral group $D_m$ is the group of symmetries of a regular $m$-gon: $m$ rotations and $m$ reflections, totalling $2m$ elements. It is generated by a rotation $\sigma$ (order $m$) and a reflection $\tau$ (order $2$), subject to the relation $\tau \sigma = \sigma^{-1}\tau$. This single relation is what makes $D_m$ non-Abelian for $m \geq 3$, and it is the source of essentially all the interesting structure in this part. Drag the polygon below to feel it.

This is why $\tau\sigma \neq \sigma\tau$: reflecting after rotating is not the same as rotating after reflecting.

4. The rotating cube: Rot(□) ≅ Sym₄

Not every group is easy to draw as a polygon. The cube has $24$ rotational symmetries — rotations that map the cube to itself without flipping it inside-out. These rotations permute the cube's four body diagonals (the lines through the centre connecting opposite vertices), and this permutation is faithful: different rotations give different permutations. Since there are $4! = 24$ permutations of four objects and $24$ rotations, the correspondence is a bijection. The rotation group of the cube is isomorphic to $\mathrm{Sym}_4$, the symmetric group on four letters.

This is our first example of an isomorphism — a structure-preserving bijection between two groups that look superficially different. It is also a preview of Cayley's theorem: every group embeds into some symmetric group $\mathrm{Sym}_n$. Manipulate the cube below to see the isomorphism in action.

We show only the orientation-preserving symmetries (rotations), because those are what form a group under composition in three dimensions. 5. Subgroups, cosets, and Lagrange

A subgroup $H$ of $G$ is a subset closed under the operation and inverses — a group in its own right, sitting inside $G$. The clock $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ lives inside $\mathbb{Z}$ as the multiples of $m$; $\mathrm{Alt}_n$ (the even permutations) lives inside $\mathrm{Sym}_n$. Subgroups are the first tool for understanding a group's internal architecture.

Given a subgroup $H \leq G$ and an element $g \in G$, the left coset $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$ is a translate of $H$ inside $G$. The cosets partition $G$ into equal-sized blocks — and this single observation, that $|G|$ equals the number of cosets times the size of each coset, is Lagrange's theorem. It is the most consequential counting principle in finite group theory.

Theorem — Lagrange

Let $G$ be a finite group and $H \leq G$ a subgroup. Then $|G| = [G : H] \cdot |H|$, where $[G:H] = |G|/|H|$ is the number of left cosets of $H$ in $G$. In particular, $|H|$ divides $|G|$, and the order of every element $g \in G$ (the smallest $k$ with $g^k = e$) divides $|G|$.

Where the picture lies

The coset tiling is exact — cosets genuinely partition $G$ into equal-sized disjoint blocks, and the counting is a theorem. What the circular arrangement suggests but does not literally mean is a geometric translation: for $\mathbb{R}^n$ with a subspace $V$, the cosets $v + V$ are literally parallel planes in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (exact). For a general finite group, cosets are algebraic translates, not geometric ones — the circle is just a convenient way to draw a finite set, not a claim about metric distance.

6. Cyclic groups, generators, and order

Lagrange's theorem has an immediate corollary that closes the loop on Part 1's clock. If $g \in G$ has order $k$ (meaning $g^k = e$ and $k$ is minimal), then $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \dots, g^{k-1}\}$ is a subgroup of order $k$, so $k \mid |G|$ by Lagrange. In particular, $g^{|G|} = e$ for every element of a finite group. This generalises Fermat's little theorem ($a^{p} \equiv a \pmod{p}$) and Euler's theorem ($a^{\varphi(m)} \equiv 1 \pmod{m}$ when $\gcd(a,m)=1$) — both are group-theoretic statements about the order of an element in $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$.

A group is cyclic if it equals $\langle g \rangle$ for some generator $g$. Every cyclic group is Abelian, and the classification is complete: the cyclic group of order $n$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and the infinite cyclic group is $\mathbb{Z}$. The subgroup lattice of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ mirrors the divisor lattice of $n$: for each $d \mid n$ there is exactly one subgroup of order $d$, namely $\langle n/d \rangle$. This is the same "atoms and molecules" structure you saw with prime factorisation in Part 1 — divisors correspond to subgroups, and the FTA controls the lattice.

7. Group actions: orbit–stabiliser and conjugacy

A group action of $G$ on a set $X$ is a homomorphism $G \to \mathrm{Sym}(X)$ — each group element permutes $X$, and composition is respected. Actions are how groups "do" things: rotate a polygon, permute roots, shuffle a deck. The fundamental counting principle for actions is the orbit–stabiliser theorem, and it is best understood as a conservation law.

Definition — Orbit and Stabiliser

For an action of $G$ on $X$ and a point $x \in X$:

Orbit: $\mathrm{Orb}(x) = \{g \cdot x : g \in G\}$ — the set of points reachable from $x$.

Stabiliser: $\mathrm{Stab}(x) = \{g \in G : g \cdot x = x\}$ — the subgroup fixing $x$.

Orbit–Stabiliser Theorem: $|G| = |\mathrm{Orb}(x)| \cdot |\mathrm{Stab}(x)|$.

The class equation reads $6 = 1 + 3 + 2$, and the identity sits alone because it commutes with everything.

The class equation is a powerful structural tool. It states that $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum [G : C_G(x_i)]$, where $Z(G)$ is the centre (elements commuting with everything) and the sum runs over representatives of non-central conjugacy classes. One consequence: every group of prime-power order has a non-trivial centre (the centre's size is divisible by $p$), which is the key to proving that such groups are solvable — a property we develop in Section 10. Another is the Sylow theorems (stated, not proved here): for each prime $p$ dividing $|G|$, there exists a subgroup of order $p^k$ (where $p^k \| |G|$), and all such subgroups are conjugate. Sylow subgroups are the "prime-factor atoms" of a group's order, and their interactions control much of finite group theory.

8. Homomorphisms, normal subgroups, and quotients

A homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$ is a function that respects the group structure: $\varphi(a \star_G b) = \varphi(a) \star_H \varphi(b)$. It is the group-theoretic instance of the categorical spine — a structure-preserving map. If $\varphi$ is bijective, it is an isomorphism, and $G \cong H$; if $G = H$, it is an endomorphism; if it is a bijective endomorphism, an automorphism. The kernel $\ker\varphi = \{g \in G : \varphi(g) = e_H\}$ and the image $\mathrm{im}\varphi = \{\varphi(g) : g \in G\}$ measure what $\varphi$ collapses and what it reaches.

The kernel is always a subgroup, but more: it is a normal subgroup, meaning $g \cdot \ker\varphi \cdot g^{-1} = \ker\varphi$ for all $g$. Equivalently, the left and right cosets of a normal subgroup $N$ coincide ($gN = Ng$), and this is exactly what makes the quotient group $G/N$ well-defined: the operation on cosets $[g] \cdot [g'] = [g \star g']$ does not depend on the chosen representatives. Normal subgroups are the group-theoretic analogue of Part 1's equivalence relations — they are the subgroups you can "divide by."

Theorem — First Isomorphism Theorem

Let $\varphi : G \to H$ be a homomorphism. Then $G / \ker\varphi \cong \mathrm{im}\varphi$. The map $\bar{\varphi}([g]) = \varphi(g)$ is a well-defined isomorphism from the quotient $G/\ker\varphi$ to the image $\mathrm{im}\varphi \leq H$.

The Third Isomorphism Theorem complements this: if $N \trianglelefteq G$ and $H \leq G$ with $N \leq H$, then $H/N \trianglelefteq G/N$ and $(G/N)/(H/N) \cong G/H$. It says that quotienting in stages is the same as quotienting once — the "cancellation" of quotients, analogous to how $(a/b)/c = a/(bc)$ for integers. Together, the isomorphism theorems are the computational engine of group theory: they let you relate a group to its quotients and subgroups without ever leaving the category of groups.

9. Direct products and finite abelian classification

Given two groups $G$ and $H$, their direct product $G \times H$ has elements $(g, h)$ and operation $(g_1, h_1) \star (g_2, h_2) = (g_1 \star_G g_2, h_1 \star_H h_2)$. It is the categorical product — the group that projects onto both factors. The order is $|G| \cdot |H|$, and $(g, e_H)$ and $(e_G, h)$ form commuting subgroups isomorphic to $G$ and $H$ respectively.

For finite Abelian groups, the direct product gives a complete classification that echoes the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic from Part 1. The primary decomposition theorem states that every finite Abelian group is a direct product of cyclic groups of prime-power order, and this decomposition is unique up to reordering. The primes are the "atoms"; the cyclic $p$-power factors are the "prime powers"; the direct product is the "multiplication." Just as $60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$, the group $\mathbb{Z}/60 \cong \mathbb{Z}/4 \times \mathbb{Z}/3 \times \mathbb{Z}/5$. The analogy with FTA is not metaphorical — it is the same theorem, restated in the language of groups.

10. Simple groups, composition series, and solvability

If normal subgroups are what you can "divide by," then the groups with no non-trivial normal subgroups are the primes of group theory. A group $G$ is simple if its only normal subgroups are $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself. The cyclic groups $\mathbb{Z}/p$ (for $p$ prime) are simple and Abelian. But there also exist non-Abelian simple groups — and their existence is what makes the theory of solvability, and ultimately the unsolvability of the quintic, possible.

A composition series for $G$ is a chain $G = G_0 \triangleright G_1 \triangleright \cdots \triangleright G_k = \{e\}$ where each $G_{i+1}$ is normal in $G_i$ and each quotient $G_i / G_{i+1}$ is simple. The quotients are the composition factors — the "atoms" of $G$. The Jordan–Hölder theorem states that these factors are unique up to isomorphism and reordering, regardless of the chosen series. This is the direct analogue of the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: a group factors into simple "atoms" in an essentially unique way.

Notice that…

$\mathrm{Sym}_3$ and $\mathbb{Z}/6$ both have order $6$ and both decompose into the same composition factors $\{\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/3\}$, yet they are not isomorphic — one is non-Abelian, the other Abelian. This is the group-theoretic analogue of isomers in chemistry: same atoms, different molecule. Jordan–Hölder says the factors are an invariant; it does not say they determine the group. The missing ingredient is how the factors are "glued" together — and for the quintic, the gluing involves $A_5$, which is simple and non-Abelian.

A group is solvable if it has a composition series in which every factor is Abelian (hence cyclic of prime order). The name comes from Galois theory: a polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is solvable. All groups of order $\leq 4$ are solvable. $\mathrm{Sym}_3$ is solvable (factors $\mathbb{Z}/3, \mathbb{Z}/2$, both Abelian). $\mathrm{Sym}_4$ is solvable (factors $\mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/3, \mathbb{Z}/2, \mathbb{Z}/2$). But $\mathrm{Sym}_5$ is not solvable, because it contains $A_5$ as a normal subgroup, and $A_5$ is simple and non-Abelian — an atom that cannot be broken further into Abelian pieces. This is the obstruction that makes the general quintic unsolvable, and it is where Part 4 will pick up the thread.

Theorem — Jordan–Hölder

Any two composition series of a finite group $G$ have the same length and the same composition factors up to isomorphism and reordering. The factors are an invariant of $G$, written $\mathrm{Comp}(G)$. However, $G$ is not determined by its composition factors alone — non-isomorphic groups can share the same factors (isomers).

Where the picture lies

The composition series and Jordan–Hölder theorem are exact — the factors are a genuine group invariant, and the towers in the figure depict real normal chains. The "atoms and molecules" metaphor is precise: simple groups are the atoms, direct/semi-direct products are the bonds, and Jordan–Hölder is the uniqueness of the atomic decomposition. What the figure does not capture is the extension problem — how the factors are assembled into the group, which is not classified by Jordan–Hölder and is in general much harder. The $A_5$ block is flagged red because it is the first non-Abelian atom, and non-Abelian atoms are the obstruction to solvability.
