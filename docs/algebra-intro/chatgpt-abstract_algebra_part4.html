Part 4 — Field Extensions & Galois Theory

A field extension is the moment algebra becomes geometry again: a larger field can be viewed as a vector space over a smaller one, and the hidden symmetries of roots become permutations constrained by equations.

1. An extension is a new dimension

Let E/F be a field extension. The key first move is familiar from linear algebra: regard E as a vector space over F. For ℚ(√2), every element has the form a+b√2, so the extension is a two-dimensional ℚ-vector space with basis {1,√2}.

Drag the blue vector or adjust the multiplier. Multiplication by c+d√2 sends a+b√2 to (ca+2db)+(da+cb)√2. In coordinates, multiplication is a linear map with matrix [[c,2d],[d,c]].

DefinitionIf E/F is finite, the degree [E:F] is the dimension of E as an F-vector space.
Where the picture liesThe plane is exact for ℚ(√2) as a two-dimensional vector space over ℚ. The grid uses real coordinates for visibility; the rational points are the exact algebraic points.

2. Minimal polynomial as the cage

Adjoin a number α to a field F. The powers 1,α,α²,… try to create more and more directions. If α is algebraic, eventually a relation traps the tower: one power becomes an F-linear combination of earlier powers.

The first nonzero polynomial relation is the minimal polynomial. Slide the power depth. The green cage marks the basis that survives; the first red node is the relation that closes the span.

DefinitionThe minimal polynomial of an algebraic element α over F is the monic irreducible polynomial of least degree in F[X] with f(α)=0. If its degree is n, then F(α)=F[α] has basis 1,α,…,α^{n-1}.
Where the picture liesThe power tower is exact for the listed algebraic elements. It does not depict transcendental elements, where the powers never close by a polynomial relation.

3. Roots in the complex plane

Factoring over a field asks which roots already live there. A splitting field is the smallest field extension that contains all roots of a polynomial. In the complex plane, the roots are visible dots; algebra asks what field must be grown so that every dot is included.

Switch between a depressed cubic x³+px+q and the clock polynomial xⁿ−1. The second mode reuses the wrap-around clock thread: roots of unity are the multiplicative clock in ℂ.

DefinitionA splitting field of f∈F[X] is a smallest extension K/F in which f factors completely into linear terms.
Where the picture liesThe plotted roots are numerical approximations for the cubic and exact unit-circle placements for xⁿ−1. The splitting-field label is conceptual: the canvas shows roots, not all field elements generated by them.

4. The Galois group permutes the roots

A field automorphism fixing F cannot move a root arbitrarily. It must preserve every algebraic relation with coefficients in F. So the Galois group acts as a subgroup of a symmetric group: permutations of roots, but only the relation-preserving ones survive.

The figure uses the biquadratic extension ℚ(√2,i)/ℚ. The roots come in two algebraic cages: x²−2 and x²+1. Flip signs inside a cage and the relation is preserved; mix cages and the permutation breaks the equations.

DefinitionFor a Galois extension E/F, Gal(E/F) is the group of field automorphisms of E that fix every element of F. It acts faithfully on the roots of any polynomial whose splitting field is E.
Where the picture liesThe relation test is exact for the displayed generators: preserving the two cages gives the four automorphisms of ℚ(√2,i) over ℚ. General Galois groups may require many more relations than the diagram can draw.

5. The Galois correspondence as mirror lattices

The Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory turns intermediate fields into subgroups, but reverses inclusion. More field means fewer automorphisms left unfixed; more subgroup means a smaller fixed field.

Click a field node. Its corresponding subgroup lights up on the right. The example is the splitting field E=ℚ(∛2,ω) of x³−2, whose Galois group is S₃.

Fundamental Theorem of Galois TheoryFor a finite Galois extension E/F, intermediate fields F⊂K⊂E correspond inclusion-reversingly to subgroups Gal(E/K)⊂Gal(E/F). Normal intermediate extensions correspond to normal subgroups.
Where the picture liesThe lattice is exact for the worked S₃ example. The vertical placement is a drawing convention for inclusion; the inclusion-reversing correspondence is the rigorous content.

6. Why five is different

Radical formulas build fields by repeatedly adjoining roots. Each such step contributes an Abelian layer. Therefore a polynomial solvable by radicals must have a solvable Galois group: its composition factors are Abelian atoms.

Slide the degree. Degrees 2, 3, and 4 have green composition towers. But a generic irreducible quintic may have Galois group S₅, whose tower hits A₅, the first non-Abelian simple atom. That red atom blocks radical solvability.

TheoremIf f∈ℚ[X] is solvable by radicals, then the Galois group of its splitting field over ℚ is solvable. For example, an irreducible quintic with Galois group S₅ is not solvable by radicals.
Where the picture liesThe solvability implication is exact in the characteristic-zero setting used here. The visual towers show composition factors, not explicit radical expressions. Degree five is not saying every quintic fails; it says generic/full-S₅ quintics fail.
Part 4 closes the series by turning the recurring threads into one circuit: clocks become roots of unity, partitions become intermediate fields, structure-preserving maps become automorphisms, and atoms become the obstruction to solving the quintic by radicals.
