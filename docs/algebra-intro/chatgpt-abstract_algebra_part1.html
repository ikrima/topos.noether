Interactive Abstract Algebra Visual Essays · Part 1

Composition, Sets, and the Structure of ℤ

Abstract algebra begins with a small gesture: two things combine to make a third. This essay keeps that gesture visible while it grows into functions, partitions, prime factorization, congruences, and the finite arithmetic worlds ℤ/mℤ.

1. Algebra begins as composition

Before groups, rings, or fields, there is a combining rule. The two input slots might hold integers, rotations, matrices, symmetries, or polynomials. Algebra asks: what structural facts about the rule survive after we forget what the objects originally were?

Figure 1 · The combining machine — a binary operation as a touchable primitive
Drag the sliders. The abstract slot-filling operation and familiar addition are the same kind of act: two inputs, one output.
Definition
A binary operation on a set S is a function S × S → S. It is a law of composition: two elements of the same world combine and stay in that world.

Paulin’s first move is to treat ordinary arithmetic as a source of structure: (ℤ,+), (ℤ,+,×), and (ℚ,+,×) are not merely collections of numbers; they are sets with operations. Abstract algebra starts by preserving the operations and asking which consequences depend only on their laws.

Where the picture lies
The machine captures closure and the idea of a binary operation exactly. It does not by itself show associativity, commutativity, identity, or inverses; those require extra tests.

2. Functions as structured matching

A function is already a kind of algebraic machine: each domain element gets exactly one output. The crucial refinements are whether different inputs collide, whether every target is hit, and whether the matching is perfect enough to reverse.

Figure 2 · Function as a matching — injective, surjective, bijective
Drag from a blue domain dot to a target dot.
When the matching is bijective, inverse arrows appear: the codomain can relabel the domain without losing information.
Definition
For f:S→T: injective means f(x)=f(y) ⇒ x=y; surjective means every t∈T is hit; bijective means both. A bijection is a perfect relabelling.

This “relabel without loss” intuition returns constantly. Later, an isomorphism will be a bijection that also preserves structure. The set-level story is the prototype.

Where the picture lies
The arrows capture ordinary functions of finite sets exactly. The inverse arrows appear only when the matching is bijective, exactly matching the theorem that a bijection has a two-sided inverse.

3. Equivalence relations are partitions

An equivalence relation says when two elements should be regarded as “the same for the question at hand.” For congruence modulo m, all integers with the same remainder become one class.

Figure 3 · Equivalence relation as coloring — same remainder mod m
Color is the equivalence class. The same set becomes a stack of disjoint colored blocks.
Definition / Theorem
An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. Its equivalence classes are non-empty, pairwise disjoint, and cover the set; conversely, any partition defines an equivalence relation. Thus: equivalence relation ⇔ partition.

Notice how little arithmetic is needed: the relation a∼b ⇔ m | (a-b) simply colors the integer line by remainder. The quotient set is the set of colors.

Where the picture lies
For a finite visible window of integers, the colored blocks are a truncated view. The partition of all of ℤ into residue classes modulo m is exact.

4. The integer line generated by 1

The integers under addition are built from a single step. Starting from 0, repeated composition by +1 and its inverse −1 walks the entire line. This is the first visual seed of “generated by one element.”

Figure 4 · ℤ generated by 1 — walking with +1 and −1
current: 0 visited in view: 1
Green marks visited integers; the amber arrow is the latest action. Repeated ±1 generates the visible part of ℤ.
Definition / Observation
The operations + : ℤ×ℤ→ℤ and × : ℤ×ℤ→ℤ obey associativity, identities, commutativity, and distributivity. Addition also has inverses. Multiplication on ℤ has identity but not inverses for most elements.

That asymmetry is why (ℤ,+) becomes the model for groups, while (ℤ,+,×) becomes the model for rings. Passing from ℤ to ℚ adds multiplicative inverses for every nonzero element.

Where the picture lies
The walk exactly shows that every displayed integer is a finite sum of 1 or −1. It cannot display all of ℤ at once, but the induction pattern is exact.

5. Primes as atoms

Under multiplication, ℤ is not generated by one element. Instead, positive integers break into prime atoms. Different factor trees may split the number in different orders, but the leaves reconcile to the same multiset of primes.

Figure 5 · Prime factorization as atoms — uniqueness through different splits
Change the split rule. The tree changes, but the prime atom bin at the bottom does not.
Theorem — Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic
Every positive integer greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes, and this factorization is unique up to reordering.

Euclid’s lemma is the hidden lock: if a prime divides a product, it divides one factor. That is what prevents two genuinely different prime atom multisets from representing the same integer.

Theorem — Infinitude of primes
There are infinitely many primes: from any finite list p₁,…,pᵣ, the number p₁⋯pᵣ+1 has a prime divisor not on the list.
Where the picture lies
The atom metaphor is exact for unique prime multisets in ℤ. The visual tree is only one possible factorization path; the leaves are the invariant.

6. The clock: congruence and ℤ/mℤ

Congruence folds the infinite integer line into a finite clock. Two integers are congruent modulo m exactly when they land on the same tick. Addition and multiplication can be performed upstairs in ℤ, then collapsed to the clock, and the answer does not depend on which representative was chosen.

Figure 6 · The clock — ℤ/mℤ — addition, multiplication, units, zero-divisors
Green residues are units. Red nonzero residues are zero-divisors. When m is prime, every nonzero tick turns green.
Definition
For m∈ℕ, a≡b mod m ⇔ m | (a-b). The residue classes form ℤ/mℤ = {[0],[1],…,[m−1]}.
Definition / Well-definedness
Addition and multiplication on residue classes are [a]+[b]=[a+b] and [a]×[b]=[ab]. These are well-defined because changing representatives does not change the resulting residue class.

Now the key structural difference appears. If m=rs is composite, then [r] and [s] can both be nonzero while [r][s]=[0]. These are zero-divisors. But when m=p is prime, every nonzero residue is coprime to p and therefore has a multiplicative inverse.

Corollary
All nonzero elements of ℤ/mℤ have multiplicative inverses if and only if m is prime. Equivalently, ℤ/pℤ is a field.
Where the picture lies
The clock exactly represents residue classes modulo m, and the green/red unit versus zero-divisor classification is exact for ℤ/mℤ. The small table is limited to visible m≤13 for readability, not mathematics.
