The Handshake Across Time

A photon, if we squint just right, is not a tiny bullet — it is a transaction between two atoms, agreed upon by a wave sent forward in time and a wave sent backward in time. Let's build one.

What you are watching is one photon — or rather, what this paper argues a photon really is. The red atom on the left is excited. It wants to shed its energy. The blue atom on the right is in its ground state, hungry for exactly that amount.

Between them, an outward-traveling retarded wave from the emitter (heading forward in time, like every wave you've ever animated) meets an inward-traveling advanced wave from the absorber (heading backward in time). Where they overlap, and only where they overlap, energy exists. That overlap — the purple ridge — is the photon. [1]

If that idea makes your raytracer-brain itch, good. It should. Let me walk you through why physicists were driven to something this strange, and how the paper by Watts & Mead patches a specific hole in an older theory. By the end I want you to feel the shape of the argument, not the algebra.

1. The bullet that has no shape

In the mental model most of us inherited, a photon is a little energy packet. It leaves atom A at the speed of light, traverses vacuum like a projectile in a first-person shooter, and eventually a raycast intersects atom B, depositing hν. Clean, causal, easy to code.

Quantum Electrodynamics — the extraordinarily accurate theory Feynman and others built in the 1940s — actually refuses to give you that picture. It computes probabilities that a photon will be detected somewhere, with breathtaking precision, but it treats the photon as a structureless point particle. It does not tell you what the photon looks like in flight. It does not tell you what "in flight" even means. [1]

Feynman was refreshingly honest about this:

"The more you see how strangely Nature behaves, the harder it is to make a model that explains how even the simplest phenomena actually work. So theoretical physics has given up on that." — R. P. Feynman, 1985 [1]

So here is the puzzle in one slider. Below is a naive "photon as bullet" simulator. Drag the absorber. Notice what the model tells you about the photon between emission and absorption.

The bullet model. Where is the photon right now?

Unfortunately, this won't really work — you can witness it by asking a physicist "where is the energy at t = halfway?" The bullet model answers "here, at position x." QED answers "the question is ill-posed." Neither answers "here is a wave shaped like this."

The paper's authors list six specific complaints against QED. I'll give you the ones that matter for our story [1]:

  • QED tells you photon probabilities but has no model of photon emission, transmission, or absorption.
  • QED requires renormalization — subtracting infinities — which even Dirac called "not sensible mathematics."
  • QED cannot cleanly explain non-local experiments: EPR, Hanbury-Brown-Twiss, Freedman-Clauser entanglement.
  • The vacuum energy predicted by QED is roughly 60 orders of magnitude larger than the cosmological constant we actually measure. This is the "worst prediction in physics."

None of this means QED is wrong. It means QED is a black box that predicts numbers without telling you what the machine inside is doing. The Watts & Mead paper is an attempt to describe the machine.

2. Two kinds of ripples

Every wave equation you've ever solved — for light, for sound, for water — has two solutions. One expands outward from the source into the future; that's the retarded solution, and it's the one we always keep. The other converges inward from the future toward the source; that's the advanced solution, and it's the one we always throw away because it seems to violate causality.

If you've written a raytracer, this feels familiar: forward path tracing shoots rays from the camera into the past to find light. Bidirectional path tracing shoots rays from lights too, and connects them. The transactional picture is doing something similar, but for causality itself.

In the demo below, the red atom emits a retarded wave (rings expanding outward, forward in time). The blue atom emits an advanced wave (rings expanding outward from the absorber, but backward in time — from its perspective, they are collapsing inward from its future). Scrub the time slider and watch each in isolation.

The retarded wave from the emitter and the advanced wave from the absorber. Individually, neither carries energy.

Here is the crucial move the paper inherits from Mead and Cramer: the vector potential A is not itself energy. It's a kind of accounting device — a memory of currents elsewhere, transported at the speed of light. It has no degrees of freedom of its own. It only becomes energetic when it multiplies against another current, at another point on its light cone. [1]

This is a much stronger statement than it sounds. It's saying: a wave alone, propagating through vacuum, carries nothing. It's a promise, not a payment.

So we have two promises: Aret from the emitter and Aadv from the absorber. To get real energy, we have to combine them. This is where the whole story turns.

3. The first guess: just add them

Cramer and Mead's 2020 paper made the most natural first guess: let the energy density in flight be proportional to Aret + Aadv — the sum of the two vector potentials. Where they constructively interfere, energy lives. Where they destructively interfere, it doesn't. [1]

You can imagine why this is appealing. It's linear, it's symmetric, and it gives you an "energy in transit" field between the two atoms.

Below, drag the distance slider. The interference pattern between the two waves is shown as it would be under the sum proposal. Watch what happens to the total energy contained in the tube between the atoms as you pull them apart.

The 2020 "sum" proposal. Total energy in the corridor is plotted on the right — it should stay equal to hν. Does it?

Unfortunately, this won't really work — you can witness this by pulling the atoms apart. The total energy in the corridor changes with distance. It grows, or shrinks, or wobbles. It doesn't conserve.

Worse, the derivation is only valid for distances below about 0.52 meters. Beyond that, the geometry of the overlap changes and the whole scheme falls apart. [1]

Two failures, then. Energy isn't conserved, and the model doesn't scale to astronomical distances. If a photon from a distant galaxy is going to travel 13 billion light-years to hit the James Webb telescope, "valid up to half a meter" is a problem.

4. The fix: multiply, don't add

Thankfully, we can solve this problem by changing one operation. Instead of the sum of the two waves, take the product. And then take the root-mean-square of that product over the oscillation cycle. This is the central proposal of Watts & Mead 2026:

energy density ∝ RMSt ( Aret · Aadv ) Equation 33 of the paper, in spirit. [1]

Two things immediately become nicer. First, since neither wave alone contains energy, and their product is only large where they overlap constructively along a light-cone-connecting path, energy is naturally localized to the corridor between the two atoms. Second, the RMS operation strips out the fast phase oscillations, leaving a slowly-varying envelope you can integrate.

In the demo below you can watch the sum-proposal energy and the product-proposal energy side by side as you slide the atoms apart. Notice which one stays flat.

Top: the field between the atoms. Bottom: the total corridor energy as d varies. The product model flatlines at hν. The sum model does not.

You may have realized that the product model does something subtly beautiful. Because Aret falls off as 1/remit and Aadv falls off as 1/rabs, their product falls off as 1/(remit · rabs). The volume of the lens-shaped overlap region between two expanding spheres grows in just the right way to compensate. The math balances. [1]

This is the same trick that makes bidirectional path tracing converge nicely: the geometry factors from both endpoints multiply, and the sampling density does the rest.

5. The shape of a photon

Before we continue, it may be worth pausing to ask: what does a single photon actually look like, under this model? The paper gives it a specific shape. It rises smoothly, oscillates at frequency ω = E/ℏ inside a soft envelope, and decays back to nothing. The rise and fall are governed by a time constant τ that we'll pin down in a moment. [1]

In the demo below you can drag the frequency and the pulse duration to sculpt individual photons. This is the "waveform" of a photon in flight — the internal wave structure that QED denies exists.

Dipole envelope green: a1(t)·a2(t), the product of excited and ground state amplitudes. Inside it: the carrier wave at the transition frequency. [1]

Notice that as the emitter transitions from excited to ground, the two amplitudes cross, and their product — the dipole envelope — peaks. That envelope is what modulates the outgoing retarded wave. It's why a photon has a finite spectral linewidth: it's not a pure sine, it's a wavepacket.

If you've ever written a shader that multiplies a carrier by a Gaussian envelope to make a wavelet, you've built exactly this. The paper's photon is a wavelet in a 3D field, moving between two localized sources.

6. Where does τ come from?

Here is a real conceptual jewel. So far we have a shape but no absolute scale. How long should a photon pulse last? What sets τ?

The paper's answer invokes Mach's Principle: the local behavior of an atom is determined by all the matter in the universe. Specifically, the emitter doesn't get to choose one absorber. Every atom in its future light cone is a candidate. Each one offers a possible transaction. The mean transition time we observe in the lab is set by summing over all of them, and matching to the transition rate that Fermi's Golden Rule already predicts. [1]

The result is a proportionality constant with no free parameters. The theory is fully pinned down by the requirement that the universe as a whole conspires to make hydrogen atoms decay at the rate we measure them decaying.

The emitter is offered many absorbers. The transaction that completes is one of them; the rate is set by all of them.

This is the sort of move that would have made Wheeler smile. It's the "absorber theory" idea he and Feynman toyed with in 1945, quietly extended to quantum mechanics. Radiation happens because the future is listening. [1]

7. From a table lamp to the Hubble sphere

There is one additional clever bit that's been hiding in plain sight. Once the model conserves energy and the constant is fixed by Mach's principle, you can ask: over what range of distances does it work?

The paper claims the answer is 26 orders of magnitude — from about 0.52 meters (the crossover where the traveling pulse geometry kicks in) up to the radius of the Hubble sphere, roughly 1.27 × 10²⁶ m. A single mathematical description covers photons crossing a room, photons from Andromeda, and photons from JADES-GS-z13-0, one of the most distant galaxies Webb has ever imaged. [1]

Slide the distance below — logarithmically — and watch the photon transit time. The model is asserting that the same equation governs every single one of these regimes.

One equation, 26 orders of magnitude. The regime boundary at ~0.52 m is where d ∝ τ gives way to d ∝ √τ. [1]

8. What this theory still cannot do

Let's zoom back out. It would be dishonest to leave you thinking the paper has replaced QED. It has not, and the authors are careful to say so. The paper explicitly lists what still lies dormant. [1]

What the paper does claim

  • A mathematical model of photon emission.
  • A mathematical model of photon absorption.
  • A mathematical model of photon transit that carries E = hν at c in a straight line.
  • Energy conservation over 26 orders of magnitude.
  • No free parameters — the constant comes from Mach.

What it still owes us

  • An explicit single-slit demonstration.
  • A double-slit demonstration (with and without which-path detection).
  • EPR, Hanbury-Brown-Twiss, Freedman-Clauser entanglement.
  • A non-singular hydrogen wavefunction (fine structure, spin-½, Lamb shift).
  • A resolution of the cosmological constant problem.
  • A mathematically consistent replacement for renormalization.

The authors call the paper "an important early step toward a future, more refined theory." That is honest. What they've done is give a mechanical picture — a picture you can, quite literally, animate in a browser — of the one specific process QED refuses to visualize: the transit of a single quantum of light between two atoms. [1]

9. A raytracer's-eye view

Given your background, I want to offer one final analogy before we close. In 2006, real-time raytracing was a battle over how to sample the incoming light at a shading point. Forward path tracing sends rays from the eye and hopes they eventually connect to an emissive surface. Light tracing sends rays from lights and hopes they connect back. Bidirectional path tracing does both and connects the paths in the middle, weighted by their contribution.

The transactional interpretation is a photon-scale bidirectional path tracer, with one twist: the "connection" is not a heuristic to reduce variance. It is the physics. The forward ray from the emitter and the backward ray from the absorber multiply, and the RMS of that product is the energy carried. There are no photons "between" the endpoints; there is only the completed handshake.

10. Return to wonder

In the 1940s, physicists made a bargain. They accepted that a photon would be a structureless point, that its trajectory would be a probability, and that its infinities would be renormalized away — in exchange for numerical predictions of unmatched accuracy. It was a good bargain. It gave us lasers, semiconductors, and the very phone or laptop this essay is likely running on.

But the bargain came with a debt. It left generations of physicists — and, quietly, generations of engineers and graphics people — with no picture of light. No shape. No mechanism. Just a formula that works.

What Watts and Mead are proposing is the beginning of a repayment. A photon, in their picture, is a standing agreement between two atoms: a wave from the past multiplied against a wave from the future, weighted so that the entire universe of possible absorbers sets the rate at which the transaction completes. It is not a bullet. It is not a wavefunction collapse. It is a handshake across time. [1]

Whether or not this is right — whether it will survive contact with the double slit, with entanglement, with the cosmological constant — is a story for future papers. What matters today is that someone tried, in a field that had officially given up. And with a browser and a few hundred lines of JavaScript, you can watch the machine turn.
