Render your first image

Install, render the Cornell box, and watch noise fall as you raise the sample count.

Source: docs/tutorials/01-first-render.mdlearning-oriented

We are going to install pt, render the Cornell box, and change one number to watch Monte Carlo noise fall away. By the end you will have three images on disk and a feel for what a sample buys you.

You will need Python 3.10 or newer, and about ten minutes. No GPU. No display.

Step 1 — Install it#

From the root of the repository:

python -m venv .venv
.venv/bin/pip install -e ".[dev]"

That pulls in NumPy, Taichi and DearPyGui. It takes a minute or two the first time.

Check that the command exists:

.venv/bin/pt --help

You should see a list of seven subcommands — render, gui, describe, edn, dump, flip, validate. We will use the first one now and meet three more in the next tutorial.

Step 2 — Render the Cornell box#

.venv/bin/pt render cornell --res 200 200 --spp 32 -o out/cornell.png

The first two lines Taichi prints are its own banner and are not ours. Then:

cornell-di: 200x200, 32 spp, 8 prims, 1 lights
  1/32 spp (  3.1%)     2.0 spp/s  32/32 spp (100.0%)    62.7 spp/s
  done in 0.51s
  png: out/cornell.png
  radiance: out/cornell_linear.npy
  meta: out/cornell.json

Open out/cornell.png. We are looking at the Cornell box: a white room with one red wall and one green wall, a small bright lamp in the ceiling, and two spheres on the floor.

Three things are worth noticing before we move on.

  • The shadows are soft. Their edges fade rather than stopping dead, because the lamp has area: some points on the floor can see all of it, some can see part of it, and some can see none.
  • The white walls are tinted. Near the red wall the floor is faintly red, and near the green wall faintly green. That is light bouncing off a coloured wall and onto a white one.
  • The image is grainy, especially in the shadows. That is the part we are about to fix.

Notice also that three files were written, not one. cornell.png is the picture; cornell_linear.npy is the same image before tone mapping, in linear radiance, which is what every measurement in this project is actually made against; cornell.json records the settings that produced them.

Step 3 — Change one number#

The grain is noise, and noise is what you get from estimating an integral with too few random samples. --spp is samples per pixel. Render the same scene with a quarter of them, then with eight times as many:

.venv/bin/pt render cornell --res 200 200 --spp 8   -o out/cornell_8.png
.venv/bin/pt render cornell --res 200 200 --spp 256 -o out/cornell_256.png

Both finish in about a second. Open the three PNGs side by side — cornell_8.png, cornell.png, cornell_256.png.

At 8 spp the shadow under the spheres is a mess of speckle. At 32 it is grainy but readable. At 256 it is smooth. The image has not changed at all between them; only our confidence in it has.

You will also notice the improvement slows down. Going 8 → 32 helps far more than 32 → 256, even though the second step adds seven times as many samples. Monte Carlo error falls with the square root of the sample count: to halve the noise you need four times the work. That single fact is why the rest of this project exists — every estimator here is an attempt to get more out of each sample rather than to take more samples.

Step 4 — Render a different estimator#

The scene we just rendered samples the light uniformly. Here is the same geometry with ReSTIR DI, which reuses samples across neighbouring pixels and across frames:

.venv/bin/pt render cornell_restir --res 200 200 --spp 32 -o out/restir.png

Compare out/restir.png with out/cornell.png — same sample count, same resolution. The shadows are noticeably cleaner.

That is the whole promise of resampling, and also the whole danger: an estimator that reuses samples can be made cleaner and wrong, and the difference is not something you can see. We will do exactly that in Catch a biased estimator.

What we did#

We rendered a scene, watched noise fall as the sample count rose, and saw a different estimator produce a cleaner image at the same cost.
