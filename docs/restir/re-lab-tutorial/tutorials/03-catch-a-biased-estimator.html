Catch a biased estimator

Render the same scene two ways, fail to tell them apart by eye, then measure the one that is wrong.

Source: docs/tutorials/03-catch-a-biased-estimator.mdlearning-oriented

Noise and bias are different failures. Noise you fix with more samples; bias you fix with mathematics. The trouble is that a biased renderer does not look broken — it looks good, often better than the correct one, because whatever it is doing wrong is usually also smoothing.

In this tutorial we will render the same scene two ways, fail to tell them apart by eye, and then catch the wrong one three separate ways with measurements.

You will need the venv, and about ten minutes — one command in this tutorial runs for roughly two of them. No display needed.

Step 1 — Render the same scene twice#

bias_demo is a scene built for this purpose. Render it under two different reuse weightings:

.venv/bin/pt render bias_demo --bias naive --spp 300 -o out/bias_naive.png
.venv/bin/pt render bias_demo --bias mis   --spp 300 -o out/bias_mis.png

Each takes a few seconds. --bias chooses how ReSTIR combines samples borrowed from neighbouring pixels: naive weights each contributing stream by M_i / ΣM_j, and mis uses the generalized balance heuristic.

Open the two PNGs and put them side by side.

They are hard to tell apart. Look closely at the pools of light on the floor and you may notice that naive has narrower, brighter cores where mis has broader, softer ones — but if you did not already know which was which, you would not be able to say which one was wrong. You might well prefer the wrong one; it is visibly less noisy.

Step 2 — Ask the harness#

.venv/bin/pt validate bias_demo --res 128 128 --spp 400 --ref-spp 1500 --no-flip

This runs for about two minutes. It builds a converged reference by brute force, then measures every estimator against it. Let us read the report a section at a time.

The verdict#

[bias] each estimator at 400 spp vs the reference (resampling M=8, sign-test stride 38px)
  uniform               unbiased (within noise)  mse=1.321e+00 expected_noise=3.273e+00 excess=-0.60x …
  RIS M=8               unbiased (within noise)  mse=1.030e+00 expected_noise=1.328e+00 excess=-0.22x …
  ReSTIR mis            unbiased (within noise)  mse=1.287e+00 expected_noise=9.958e-01 excess=+0.29x …
  ReSTIR pairwise       unbiased (within noise)  mse=1.292e+00 expected_noise=9.705e-01 excess=+0.33x …
  ReSTIR naive                           BIASED  mse=2.901e+00 expected_noise=6.242e-01 excess=+3.65x …

  1/M vs MIS: naive is 0.569x the energy of mis, excess +3.65x vs +0.29x
  -> the MIS weighting is doing necessary work here

excess is the part of the error that both estimators' noise cannot account for. E[MSE] = var(estimator) + var(reference) + bias², so subtracting the two variances leaves the bias squared — which is why the report needs to know how noisy the reference is, not just the estimator.

naive is missing 43% of the scene's energy (0.569x). That is not a subtle defect. And you could not see it.

The convergence curves#

[convergence] MSE vs spp. A curve that FLATTENS has hit its bias^2 floor;
              one still falling at ~ -1 is converging to the truth.
  uniform            slope=-0.74 tail=-0.57 plateau=  1.1x floor  ok      8:2.46e+01  32:7.94e+00  128:2.62e+00  512:1.19e+00
  ReSTIR mis         slope=-0.80 tail=-0.39 plateau=  1.0x floor  ok      8:2.74e+01  32:7.73e+00  128:1.85e+00  512:1.08e+00
  ReSTIR naive       slope=-0.22 tail=+0.01 plateau=  2.6x floor  BIASED  8:7.66e+00  32:3.24e+00  128:2.81e+00  512:2.84e+00

Read the four numbers on the right of the naive row: 7.66, 3.24, 2.81, 2.84. The error falls, and then it stops falling — at 512 samples it is no better than at 128. It has hit a floor, and the floor is the bias squared. The correct estimators keep descending toward the reference's own noise (plateau = 1.0x floor); naive flattens 2.6× above it.

This is a second, independent detection. It does not need the run to converge, only for the curve to have turned over.

The joke at the end#

[variance] per-pixel variance of a SINGLE frame, 6 seeds, 90th percentile.
  uniform            var(p90)=7.583e+00   (the baseline)
  RIS M=8            var(p90)=6.862e+00    1.11x better than uniform
  ReSTIR mis         var(p90)=3.925e+00    1.93x better than uniform
  ReSTIR pairwise    var(p90)=2.913e+00    2.60x better than uniform
  ReSTIR naive       var(p90)=4.819e-01   15.74x better than uniform

The wrong estimator is the cleanest image in the report — sixteen times less noisy than uniform sampling, four times less noisy than the correct weighting.

That is the entire lesson. If you were choosing an estimator by looking at renders, or by measuring noise, you would choose naive without hesitating. It throws away 43% of the light and it is beautiful.

Step 3 — Notice the row that does not fit#

You will also have seen this in the convergence section:

  RIS M=8            slope=+0.13 tail=+1.16 plateau=  7.7x floor  BIASED

RIS is not biased — it is flagged here because its error on this scene is heavy-tailed: a handful of very bright pixels dominate the mean squared error, so one realization can land far above its own expectation and the fitted slope goes the wrong way. The [bias] section, which compares against both variances, correctly reports it as unbiased on the same run.

This is worth seeing rather than hiding. MSE is the fragile statistic in this harness, and the way it fails is by producing a confident wrong answer in either direction. The project has a recorded case where the same effect made the detector report "unbiased" for an estimator losing a tenth of the scene's light — see A scene can be pathological in a way that breaks the statistics in the evidence log, along with the fix, which was to change the scene rather than the statistic.

Step 4 — Understand what makes this scene special#

Try the same measurement on the ordinary Cornell box:

.venv/bin/pt validate cornell_restir --res 96 96 --spp 200 --ref-spp 600 --no-flip

naive and mis come out indistinguishable. The bias is not there.

Spatial reuse borrows samples from neighbouring pixels, so the intuitive place to look for its failure is a geometry discontinuity — an edge, a corner, a shadow boundary. That is the wrong place: the geometric similarity gate already rejects those neighbours, so it is doing, crudely, the job the MIS weighting does properly.

The bias needs neighbours that pass the gate — same surface, same normal, same depth — and yet disagree about which samples are possible at all. bias_demo gets that with lights standing perpendicular to a flat floor: cos_light flips sign along a razor-sharp line across the plane, so two adjacent pixels are geometrically identical while one assigns zero probability to samples the other can produce.

Building that scene was harder than writing the detector.

What we did#

We produced a clean, beautiful, wrong image, and then established that it was wrong three independent ways: excess error over the combined noise floors, a convergence curve that flattened above the reference's noise instead of at it, and a stable energy deficit that did not shrink with more samples.

Two things to carry forward.

Low variance is not correctness. It is the reason this project has a statistical harness at all. Every other kind of bug here can be found by looking at a render; this one cannot.

Which statistic you measure decides whether you can see the effect. MSE found the defect on this scene. One bounce further out — on the indirect term, which is about 11% of a Cornell box — it does not: the MSE verdict reads "unbiased (within noise)" for estimators that the energy ratio resolves as broken at tens of sigma.
