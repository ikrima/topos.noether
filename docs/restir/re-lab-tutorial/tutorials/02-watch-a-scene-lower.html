Watch a scene lower

Follow one scene through every level of the pipeline, as text you can diff. No GPU, no display, a few seconds.

Source: docs/tutorials/02-watch-a-scene-lower.mdlearning-oriented

A scene here is not a config file that a renderer reads. It is a program that gets lowered — transformed step by step into more concrete forms until it reaches executable kernels — and every one of those intermediate forms is something you can print, save and diff.

In this tutorial we will look at one scene at three different levels, and then diff two scenes against each other to find the exact point where the estimator changes.

You will need the venv from the previous tutorial, and about ten minutes. Nothing here renders an image, so there is no GPU and no waiting.

Step 1 — Read the scene as its author wrote it#

.venv/bin/pt edn cornell

This prints the scene as canonical EDN — the surface syntax, cleaned up. The first lines look like this:

(scene
  {:name "cornell-di"

   :settings {:seed 1234 :tonemap :reinhard :exposure 0}

   :integrator {:type :direct-lighting
                 :light-sampling :uniform
                 :spp 256
                 :max-bounces 1}

   :camera {:type :pinhole
             :eye [278 273 -800]
             :look-at [278 273 -799]
             :up [0 1 0]
             :fov 39.3
             :resolution [512 512]
             :near 0.1 :far 5000}

Two things here will matter later.

:type says which transport we are simulating — one bounce of light, or many. :light-sampling says how the light is sampled. They are separate axes, and they are separate on purpose: you can hold one fixed and vary the other.

:seed is not decoration. Every random number this renderer draws is a pure hash of (pixel, sample, bounce, dimension, seed) — there is no random number generator with hidden state anywhere in the system. Change the seed and you get a different but equally valid image; keep it and you get the same image, every time, on any machine, no matter how the work was scheduled.

But notice what you did not see. Compare the output above with the actual source in pt/scenes/cornell.edn and you will find the file is not the same text. The printed version has resolved names to indices, filled in defaults you never wrote, computed the camera basis from look-at and up, and connected each light to its geometry in both directions. What pt edn prints is the scene after five jobs have been done to it — resolve, default, normalize, stitch, expand — which is the level the rest of the system consumes.

Step 2 — Read the schedule that will run#

Two levels further down, the scene has become a graph of passes and then a flat schedule. Print it:

.venv/bin/pt describe cornell
ExecutionPlan: 4 steps, 8 resources -> 8 physical buffers
  schedule:
    0: GeneratePrimaryRays          reads[-] writes[primary_rays] dirty[camera,spp]
    1: Intersect                    reads[primary_rays] writes[hits, aov_normal, aov_albedo, aov_depth] dirty[camera,scene,spp]
    2: ShadeDI[uniform]             reads[hits] writes[radiance_accum, moment2] dirty[camera,scene,spp]
    3: ToneMap                      reads[radiance_accum, aov_normal, aov_albedo, aov_depth] writes[framebuffer] dirty[aov,camera,exposure,scene,spp]
  resources:
    slot  0  aov_normal             [1,3]
    slot  1  aov_albedo             [1,3]
    slot  2  aov_depth              [1,3]
    slot  3  framebuffer            [3,3]
    slot  4  hits                   [1,2]
    slot  5  moment2                persistent
    slot  6  radiance_accum         persistent
    slot  7  primary_rays           [0,1]

Read it as four sentences. Shoot a ray through every pixel. Find what each ray hits. Shade the hits. Tone-map the result to a framebuffer.

Each pass states what it reads and what it writes, and that declaration is enforced — a pass that reaches for a buffer it did not declare fails when the graph is built, not on some later frame. The dirty[…] list is what makes the interactive shell cheap: drag the exposure slider and only the passes that declared exposure re-run, so every accumulated sample survives.

The numbers in brackets next to each resource are its lifetime — the first and last step that needs it. hits is [1,2]: written at step 1, last read at step 2, dead afterwards. persistent means the buffer outlives the frame.

Step 3 — Diff two estimators#

Here is where it pays off. Print the schedule for the ReSTIR version of the same room and compare:

diff <(.venv/bin/pt describe cornell     2>/dev/null) \
     <(.venv/bin/pt describe cornell_restir 2>/dev/null)
<     2: ShadeDI[uniform]             reads[hits] writes[radiance_accum, moment2] …
---
>     2: ReSTIR.InitialAndTemporal    reads[hits, reservoir_prev, hits_prev] writes[reservoir_temporal] …
>     3: ReSTIR.Spatial               reads[hits, reservoir_temporal] writes[reservoir_curr] …
>     4: ReSTIR.Resolve               reads[hits, reservoir_curr] writes[radiance_accum, moment2, aov_reservoir] …

Steps 0 and 1 are identical. Step 3's tone map is identical. One shading node has expanded into three passes and some persistent reservoirs, and nothing else in the renderer changed. That is the claim the architecture makes about how these estimators relate to one another, and here it is as a diff rather than as a paragraph.

Look at the two reuse passes closely:

ReSTIR.Spatial    reads[hits, reservoir_temporal]  writes[reservoir_curr]

It reads one buffer and writes a different one. That is not an accident and not a style choice. Spatial reuse means every pixel looks at its neighbours, so if the pass read and wrote the same buffer while running in parallel, each pixel would see an arbitrary mixture of already-updated and not-yet-updated neighbours. Two buffers cost memory and make the race impossible.

Step 4 — Look at the buffers themselves#

The last level is the data. Render a small image and write every cooked buffer to disk:

.venv/bin/pt dump cornell --res 64 64 --spp 8 --dir out/artifacts
wrote 17 arrays to out/artifacts
  aov_albedo
  aov_depth
  aov_normal
  framebuffer
  hits.light_id
  hits.material_id
  hits.n
  hits.p
  …

These are plain .npy files. Load one and look at it:

.venv/bin/python -c "
import numpy as np
n = np.load('out/artifacts/hits.n.npy')
print(n.shape, n.dtype)
print('surface normal at the centre pixel:', n[32, 32])
"

Every intermediate the renderer produced is on disk, in a format anything can read. Nothing is hidden inside the engine.

What we did#

We looked at one scene at three levels — elaborated source, compiled schedule, cooked buffers — and used a diff between two of those levels to locate the single point where two estimators differ.

The pipeline has more levels than the three we printed. In full it is:

cornell.edn  →  forms  →  SceneAST  →  SemanticIR  →  RawGraph  →  ExecutionPlan  →  kernels
       read      macro-expand  analyze    elaborate    declare      compile        execute

pt edn printed the SemanticIR, pt describe printed the ExecutionPlan, and pt dump printed what the kernels produced. The two levels with no CLI of their own are dumped by docs/explainer/capture_levels.py, and the interactive explainer has a scrubber that steps through all seven.
