00

Craft lost to theory in 1873

In 1665 Robert Hooke published Micrographia with a compound microscope — two lenses, the better architecture. Around 1674 Antoni van Leeuwenhoek, grinding single beads of glass by hand, saw bacteria. For roughly a century and a half no compound microscope matched him. The better design lost to the better craftsman, because nobody could yet calculate what a lens would do.

That ended in Jena. Ernst Abbe derived the mathematics of image formation, Otto Schott melted glasses to those equations, Carl Zeiss built to specification, and August Köhler solved illumination. Optical design moved, in the words of this workshop's own slides, from trial-and-error craftsmanship to precise mathematical and scientific design.

The inheritance problem

You did not inherit van Leeuwenhoek's problem. You inherited a theory-built instrument, and with it a new failure mode: the microscope no longer breaks when you use it wrongly.

It quietly returns a plausible image. The error does not appear as a fault. It appears as a finding.

Not one of these produces an error message. That is the entire argument for theory: without it you cannot tell an artifact from a discovery, because they look identical.

01

The image is built from diffracted light. Delete the light, delete the specimen.

Abbe's 1873 result: the objective's back focal plane holds the diffraction pattern of the specimen, and the image is the interference of whatever orders survive the trip through the pupil. This is a real Fourier optical system, so it can be computed exactly — object, transform, pupil, inverse transform.

Click the bright spots in the middle panel to block diffraction orders. The specimen never changes. Only the light you allow to reach the image does.

02

The most common thing everyone does wrong

Contrast is low, so you close the condenser aperture diaphragm. It works — contrast rises immediately. What the equations say you also bought: a smaller illumination cone, a narrower band of transmitted frequencies, and interference fringes at every edge. This is computed by summing over condenser source points, which is what partial coherence physically is.

Meanwhile fine detail collapses toward zero: the striations inside the cells are simply gone from the record. One knob, both effects, and only the flattering one is visible.

At the scope

Set the aperture diaphragm by looking, not by taste. Remove an eyepiece, sight the objective's back aperture, and close the condenser iris until it fills about 70–80% of that disc. Then leave it. Reset it on every objective change, because NAobj changed and S changed with it.

04

Undersampling does not blur. It invents.

The left panel is the optical image your objective forms — already band-limited by NA, and as good as the light can make it. The right panel is what your detector records at the pixel size you chose. Past the Nyquist limit the detector does not simply lose the fine detail; it folds that detail down into coarse periodic structure that looks like a real specimen feature.

On the frequency ramp, drag the pixel size up slowly and watch the right-hand side of the recorded panel. The stripes do not fade out; they reverse and re-form into wide bands. That is aliasing. Those bands have a spacing, they are reproducible, and they mean nothing. Then push the other way and note the cost you cannot see here: every extra pixel is more dose on a live cell for no additional information.

At the scope

Work the number out before you acquire, not after. Divide the resolution limit by about 2.3, then multiply by total magnification to get the camera pixel you need — or set confocal zoom and frame size until the software's reported pixel size matches. Binning 2×2 doubles the pixel and can drop you straight below Nyquist.

Light microscopy — bench card
Set up in this order, every session
Köhler illumination — six steps
1Focus the specimen with a mid-power objective at its correct working distance.
2Close the field diaphragm until its edge is visible in the field of view.
3Raise or lower the condenser until that edge is sharply focused.
4Centre the condenser with the two centring screws until the edge is concentric.
5Open the field diaphragm just past the edge of the field — no further.
6Remove an eyepiece, sight the objective's back aperture, and close the condenser aperture diaphragm to 70–80% of that disc.
Redo steps 3–6 on every objective change. The illumination cone that was right for NA 0.30 is wrong for NA 1.40.

NA can never exceed the index of the medium between specimen and lens. A dry lens has a hard ceiling below 1.0, and no amount of magnification moves it.
Choosing contrast
Stained or pigmented → brightfield. Set Köhler first; most “bad” brightfield is bad illumination.
Unstained thin cells in culture → phase contrast. The bright halo at every boundary is the phase plate, not a membrane.
Unstained, thick, or in plastic → DIC needs glass and no birefringence; use Hoffman modulation in plastic dishes.
Need apparent relief → DIC, but the relief is optical path gradient. Never measure height from it.
Small scatterers, dilute → darkfield. Bright on black; not quantitative.
Labelled targets → fluorescence. Now bit depth and saturation decide whether the numbers survive.
Before you call it data
No saturated pixels anywhere you intend to measure.
Identical exposure, gain and illumination across every condition being compared.
Dark and flat-field corrected, 12-bit or better, saved lossless.
Adjustments applied to the whole image, never to one region or one channel alone.
Every processing step written down, and the raw file kept.
